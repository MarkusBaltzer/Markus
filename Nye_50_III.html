01 Itô's formula
For \(f\in C^2\), Itô's formula gives \(\mathrm df(B_t)=\) ?
The Itô correction is the second-order term \(\tfrac12 f''\,\mathrm d\langle B\rangle=\tfrac12 f''\,\mathrm dt\), since \((\mathrm dB_t)^2=\mathrm dt\). Hence \(\mathrm df(B_t)=f'(B_t)\,\mathrm dB_t+\tfrac12 f''(B_t)\,\mathrm dt\).
02 Itô's formula
Applying Itô's formula to \(f(x)=x^2\), \(\mathrm d(B_t^2)=\) ?
With \(f'=2x,\;f''=2\): \(\mathrm d(B_t^2)=2B_t\,\mathrm dB_t+\tfrac12\cdot2\,\mathrm dt=2B_t\,\mathrm dB_t+\mathrm dt\). Integrating recovers \(\int_0^t B_s\,\mathrm dB_s=\tfrac12(B_t^2-t)\).
03 Itô's formula
From \(\mathrm d(B_t^3)=3B_t^2\,\mathrm dB_t+3B_t\,\mathrm dt\), the value of \(E[B_t^3]\) is:
Taking expectations, the \(\mathrm dB\)-integral is a martingale (mean \(0\)) and \(E\!\int_0^t 3B_s\,\mathrm ds=3\int_0^t E[B_s]\,\mathrm ds=0\). So \(E[B_t^3]=0\), consistent with the symmetry \(B_t\sim-B_t\).
04 Itô's formula
For a time-dependent \(f(t,x)\in C^{1,2}\), \(\mathrm df(t,B_t)=\) ?
The time derivative contributes an ordinary \(f_t\,\mathrm dt\) term; the spatial part carries both the martingale term \(f_x\,\mathrm dB_t\) and the Itô correction \(\tfrac12 f_{xx}\,\mathrm dt\). The drift is thus \((f_t+\tfrac12 f_{xx})\,\mathrm dt\).
05 Itô's formula
By Itô, the process \(Y_t=e^{B_t-t/2}\) satisfies \(\mathrm dY_t=\) ? (so it is a martingale).
With \(f(t,x)=e^{x-t/2}\): \(f_t=-\tfrac12 f,\;f_x=f,\;f_{xx}=f\). The drift \(f_t+\tfrac12 f_{xx}=-\tfrac12 f+\tfrac12 f=0\) cancels, leaving \(\mathrm dY_t=Y_t\,\mathrm dB_t\) — a driftless (hence local, and here true) martingale.
06 Itô's formula
The process \(M_t=e^{t/2}\sin(B_t)\) is a local martingale. Itô gives \(\mathrm dM_t=\) ?
With \(f=e^{t/2}\sin x\): \(f_t=\tfrac12 e^{t/2}\sin x,\;f_{xx}=-e^{t/2}\sin x\), so the drift \(f_t+\tfrac12 f_{xx}=0\). Only \(f_x\,\mathrm dB_t=e^{t/2}\cos(B_t)\,\mathrm dB_t\) survives (compare the exponential-tilt trick in 4.1(i)).
07 Itô's formula
For an Itô process \(\mathrm dX_t=\mu_t\,\mathrm dt+\sigma_t\,\mathrm dB_t\), the chain rule reads \(\mathrm df(X_t)=\) ?
The Itô correction uses \(\mathrm d\langle X\rangle_t=\sigma_t^2\,\mathrm dt\), so \(\mathrm df(X_t)=f'(X_t)\,\mathrm dX_t+\tfrac12 f''(X_t)\sigma_t^2\,\mathrm dt\). Only the diffusion coefficient enters the second-order term; the drift does not.
08 Product rule & Itô table
The Itô product rule for two Itô processes is \(\mathrm d(X_tY_t)=\) ?
The ordinary Leibniz rule picks up the covariation correction \(\mathrm d\langle X,Y\rangle_t\). This is Itô's formula applied to \((x,y)\mapsto xy\), whose only nonzero second derivative is \(\partial_{xy}=1\).
09 Product rule & Itô table
In the Itô multiplication table, \((\mathrm dB_t)^2\) equals:
The rules are \((\mathrm dB)^2=\mathrm dt\), \(\mathrm dB\,\mathrm dt=0\), \((\mathrm dt)^2=0\), encoding \(\langle B\rangle_t=t\) and the vanishing covariation of a finite-variation term.
10 Product rule & Itô table
Using the product rule, \(\mathrm d(tB_t)=\) ?
Here \(X_t=t\) has finite variation, so \(\langle t,B\rangle=0\) and \(\mathrm d(tB_t)=t\,\mathrm dB_t+B_t\,\mathrm dt\). This underlies the martingale \(tB_t-\int_0^t B_u\,\mathrm du\) from 5.3(b)(ii).
11 Product rule & Itô table
Integrating \(\mathrm d(tB_t)=t\,\mathrm dB_t+B_t\,\mathrm dt\) gives the by-parts identity \(\int_0^t s\,\mathrm dB_s=\) ?
Integrating from \(0\) to \(t\): \(tB_t=\int_0^t s\,\mathrm dB_s+\int_0^t B_s\,\mathrm ds\), so \(\int_0^t s\,\mathrm dB_s=tB_t-\int_0^t B_s\,\mathrm ds\). Stochastic integration by parts trades a \(\mathrm dB\)-integral for a pathwise one.
12 Product rule & Itô table
For two independent Brownian motions \(B^{(1)},B^{(2)}\), the covariation \(\langle B^{(1)},B^{(2)}\rangle_t\) equals:
Independence forces \(\langle B^{(1)},B^{(2)}\rangle_t=0\) (Proposition 4.16), which is exactly what kills the cross-terms when checking whether pairs like \((W^{(2)},W^{(4)})\) form a 2-d Brownian motion in 6.1(c).
13 Geometric BM & \(\mathcal E(M)\)
The solution of \(\mathrm dS_t=\mu S_t\,\mathrm dt+\sigma S_t\,\mathrm dB_t,\;S_0=s\), is:
Apply Itô to \(\log S_t\): \(\mathrm d\log S_t=(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2)\,\mathrm dt+\sigma\,\mathrm dB_t\). Exponentiating gives \(S_t=s\,e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t+\sigma B_t}\). The \(-\sigma^2/2\) is the Itô correction of the log.
14 Geometric BM & \(\mathcal E(M)\)
For \(S_t=s\,e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t+\sigma B_t}\), the mean \(E[S_t]\) equals:
Since \(E[e^{\sigma B_t}]=e^{\sigma^2 t/2}\), the \(-\sigma^2/2\) and \(+\sigma^2/2\) cancel: \(E[S_t]=s\,e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t}\,e^{\sigma^2 t/2}=s\,e^{\mu t}\). The mean grows at the drift rate \(\mu\).
15 Geometric BM & \(\mathcal E(M)\)
The geometric Brownian motion \(S_t=s\,e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t+\sigma B_t}\) is a martingale if and only if:
The SDE is \(\mathrm dS_t=\mu S_t\,\mathrm dt+\sigma S_t\,\mathrm dB_t\); it has zero drift (hence is a local, and here true, martingale) exactly when \(\mu=0\). Equivalently, \(E[S_t]=s\,e^{\mu t}\) is constant iff \(\mu=0\).
16 Geometric BM & \(\mathcal E(M)\)
The Doléans–Dade exponential \(\mathcal E(M)\) is defined as the unique solution of:
\(\mathcal E(M)_t=\exp\!\big(M_t-\tfrac12\langle M\rangle_t\big)\) solves the linear SDE \(\mathrm dZ_t=Z_t\,\mathrm dM_t\) with \(Z_0=1\). The Itô correction inside the exponent is exactly what makes \(\mathcal E(M)\) a local martingale (Proposition 5.11).
17 Geometric BM & \(\mathcal E(M)\)
Compute \(\operatorname{var}\!\big(e^{\sigma B_t-\sigma^2 t/2}\big)\).
The mean is \(1\) (it is a martingale). For the second moment, \(Z_t^2=e^{2\sigma B_t-\sigma^2 t}=\mathcal E(2\sigma B)_t\,e^{\sigma^2 t}\), so \(E[Z_t^2]=e^{\sigma^2 t}\). Hence \(\operatorname{var}=e^{\sigma^2 t}-1\).
18 Geometric BM & \(\mathcal E(M)\)
For \(S\) a geometric BM with parameters \((\mu,\sigma)\), the log-return \(\mathrm d(\log S_t)\) equals:
With \(f(x)=\log x\): \(f'=1/x,\;f''=-1/x^2\), so \(\mathrm d\log S_t=\tfrac{1}{S_t}\mathrm dS_t-\tfrac12\tfrac{1}{S_t^2}\sigma^2 S_t^2\,\mathrm dt=(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2)\,\mathrm dt+\sigma\,\mathrm dB_t\). The log drifts slower than the level by \(\sigma^2/2\).
19 Linear SDEs & OU
The solution of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck SDE \(\mathrm dX_t=-\theta X_t\,\mathrm dt+\sigma\,\mathrm dB_t\) is:
Multiply by the integrating factor \(e^{\theta t}\): \(\mathrm d(e^{\theta t}X_t)=e^{\theta t}\sigma\,\mathrm dB_t\). Integrating and dividing back gives \(X_t=X_0e^{-\theta t}+\sigma\int_0^t e^{-\theta(t-s)}\,\mathrm dB_s\).
20 Linear SDEs & OU
For the OU process above (deterministic \(X_0\)), the mean \(E[X_t]\) equals:
The Itô integral has mean \(0\), so \(E[X_t]=X_0e^{-\theta t}\): the mean reverts exponentially to \(0\) at rate \(\theta\).
21 Linear SDEs & OU
The variance \(\operatorname{var}(X_t)\) of the OU process (deterministic \(X_0\)) is:
By the Itô isometry, \(\operatorname{var}(X_t)=\sigma^2\!\int_0^t e^{-2\theta(t-s)}\,\mathrm ds=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\big(1-e^{-2\theta t}\big)\). The deterministic integrand makes \(X_t\) Gaussian (cf. 6.4).
22 Linear SDEs & OU
As \(t\to\infty\), the stationary (long-run) variance of the OU process is:
Letting \(t\to\infty\), \(e^{-2\theta t}\to0\), so \(\operatorname{var}(X_t)\to\sigma^2/(2\theta)\). The OU process has a Gaussian stationary law \(N\!\big(0,\sigma^2/(2\theta)\big)\) — unlike Brownian motion it does not diffuse to infinity.
23 Linear SDEs & OU
Which integrating factor solves the OU SDE \(\mathrm dX_t=-\theta X_t\,\mathrm dt+\sigma\,\mathrm dB_t\)?
Take \(Y_t=e^{\theta t}X_t\). By the product rule \(\mathrm dY_t=\theta e^{\theta t}X_t\,\mathrm dt+e^{\theta t}\mathrm dX_t=e^{\theta t}\sigma\,\mathrm dB_t\), which removes the drift and leaves a pure stochastic integral.
24 Girsanov
Under the measure with density \(\exp\!\big(\int_0^t\theta_s\,\mathrm dB_s-\tfrac12\int_0^t\theta_s^2\,\mathrm ds\big)\), which process is a standard Brownian motion?
Girsanov's theorem: with density \(\mathcal E(\int\theta\,\mathrm dB)\), the process \(W_t=B_t-\int_0^t\theta_s\,\mathrm ds\) is a Brownian motion under the new measure \(Q\). The change of measure adds an absolutely continuous drift \(\int\theta\,\mathrm ds\).
25 Girsanov
Which condition guarantees that \(\mathcal E\!\big(\int\theta\,\mathrm dB\big)\) is a true martingale (so the change of measure is valid)?
The Novikov condition \(E[\exp(\tfrac12\int_0^T\theta_s^2\,\mathrm ds)]<\infty\) is sufficient (Theorem 5.23); it upgrades the local martingale \(\mathcal E\) to a genuine martingale, exactly the tool used in 6.2(b).
26 Girsanov
Under an equivalent change of measure via Girsanov, which quantity is left unchanged?
Girsanov shifts the drift but leaves the quadratic variation \(\langle B\rangle_t=t\) invariant — covariation is a pathwise (a.s.) object, and equivalent measures agree on null sets. Only the drift is reweighted.
27 Girsanov
To turn \(S_t=s\,e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t+\sigma B_t}\) into a martingale under an equivalent measure (with \(W=B-\int\theta\)), the constant Girsanov kernel \(\theta\) is:
Writing \(B_t=W_t+\theta t\), the drift becomes \(\mu+\sigma\theta\); a driftless GBM needs \(\mu+\sigma\theta=0\), i.e. \(\theta=-\mu/\sigma\). This is (minus) the market price of risk. With a rate \(r\), one instead sets \(\theta=(r-\mu)/\sigma\).
28 Girsanov
If \(P\) and \(Q\) are equivalent measures, a \(P\)-Brownian motion becomes, under \(Q\):
It remains a continuous semimartingale with the same quadratic variation \(t\), decomposing under \(Q\) as a \(Q\)-Brownian motion plus a finite-variation drift. This is the content of Girsanov's theorem.
29 Feynman–Kac & BS PDE
For \(\mathrm dX=\mu(X)\,\mathrm dt+\sigma(X)\,\mathrm dB\), the function \(u(t,x)=E[g(X_T)\mid X_t=x]\) solves:
Feynman–Kac: apply Itô to \(u(t,X_t)\); requiring the drift to vanish (so \(u(t,X_t)\) is a martingale) gives the backward PDE \(u_t+\mu u_x+\tfrac12\sigma^2 u_{xx}=0\) with terminal data \(u(T,\cdot)=g\).
30 Feynman–Kac & BS PDE
The Black–Scholes PDE for an option value \(V(t,S)\) is:
\(V_t+rSV_S+\tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2 V_{SS}-rV=0\). It is Feynman–Kac for the discounted claim under the risk-neutral dynamics \(\mathrm dS=rS\,\mathrm dt+\sigma S\,\mathrm dB^Q\); the \(-rV\) term is the discounting.
31 Feynman–Kac & BS PDE
The Black–Scholes PDE arises from requiring that which process has zero drift?
Under \(Q\) the discounted value \(e^{-rt}V(t,S_t)\) must be a martingale. Applying Itô and setting the \(\mathrm dt\)-coefficient to zero yields the BS PDE; the \(\mathrm dB\)-coefficient gives the delta hedge \(V_S\).
32 Feynman–Kac & BS PDE
For the discounted expectation \(u(t,x)=E[e^{-r(T-t)}g(X_T)\mid X_t=x]\), the Feynman–Kac PDE gains which extra term versus the undiscounted case?
Discounting inserts a zeroth-order killing term: \(u_t+\mu u_x+\tfrac12\sigma^2 u_{xx}-ru=0\). It is exactly the \(-rV\) term of the Black–Scholes equation.
33 Feynman–Kac & BS PDE
For \(X=B\) (so \(\mu=0,\sigma=1\)), \(u(t,x)=E[g(B_T)\mid B_t=x]\) solves the backward heat equation:
Setting \(\mu=0,\sigma=1\) in Feynman–Kac gives \(u_t+\tfrac12 u_{xx}=0\), the backward heat equation with terminal data \(g\). The forward transition density of \(B\) solves the adjoint (forward) heat equation.
34 Risk-neutral pricing
The arbitrage-free time-\(0\) price of a claim paying \(H\) at \(T\) (constant rate \(r\)) is:
Discount under the risk-neutral (equivalent martingale) measure \(Q\): \(V_0=e^{-rT}E^Q[H]\). The expectation is under \(Q\), not the physical measure \(P\).
35 Risk-neutral pricing
Under the risk-neutral measure \(Q\), the stock follows \(\mathrm dS_t=\;\underline{\phantom{xx}}\;\mathrm dt+\sigma S_t\,\mathrm dB_t^Q\). The blank is:
Requiring the discounted stock \(e^{-rt}S_t\) to be a \(Q\)-martingale forces the drift to be \(rS_t\). Girsanov reweights the real-world drift \(\mu\) down to \(r\), the risk-free rate.
36 Risk-neutral pricing
The Black–Scholes price of a European call with strike \(K\) has the form:
\(C=S_0\Phi(d_1)-Ke^{-rT}\Phi(d_2)\) with \(d_{1,2}=\frac{\log(S_0/K)+(r\pm\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T}\). It is \(e^{-rT}E^Q[(S_T-K)^+]\) computed against the lognormal law of \(S_T\).
37 Risk-neutral pricing
The (first) fundamental theorem of asset pricing states that absence of arbitrage is equivalent to the existence of:
No arbitrage \(\Leftrightarrow\) an equivalent martingale measure exists. Uniqueness of that measure is a separate property, equivalent to market completeness (the second fundamental theorem).
38 Time change & DDS
By the Dambis–Dubins–Schwarz theorem, a continuous local martingale \(M\) (with \(\langle M\rangle_\infty=\infty\)) can be written as:
DDS: \(M_t=B_{\langle M\rangle_t}\) for a Brownian motion \(B\) run on the clock \(\langle M\rangle_t\). Every continuous local martingale is a Brownian motion in disguise, time-changed by its own quadratic variation.
39 Time change & DDS
The Gaussian martingale \(I(f)_t=\int_0^t f(s)\,\mathrm dB_s\) is a Brownian motion run on which clock?
Its quadratic variation is \(\langle I(f)\rangle_t=\int_0^t f(s)^2\,\mathrm ds=:g(t)\), so \(I(f)_t=W_{g(t)}\) and \(I(f)_t\sim N(0,g(t))\) — the Gaussian structure of 6.4 seen through time change.

47 Representation & BDG
The martingale representation theorem says any square-integrable \(\mathcal F^B\)-martingale \(M\) can be written as:
\(M_t=M_0+\int_0^t H_s\,\mathrm dB_s\) for a unique adapted \(H\) with \(E\!\int_0^t H_s^2\,\mathrm ds<\infty\). Brownian martingales have no drift part — they are pure stochastic integrals. This is what makes the market complete.
48 Representation & BDG
The martingale representation theorem (as stated) requires \(M\) to be adapted to:
The representation holds for martingales of the Brownian filtration \(\mathcal F^B\) (suitably completed). In a larger filtration a martingale can carry extra randomness not driven by \(B\), and the representation may fail.
49 Representation & BDG
The Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequalities compare \(E\big[\sup_{s\le t}|M_s|^p\big]\) (up to constants) with:
BDG: \(c_p\,E[\langle M\rangle_t^{p/2}]\le E[\sup_{s\le t}|M_s|^p]\le C_p\,E[\langle M\rangle_t^{p/2}]\) for \(p>0\). It bounds the running maximum by (a power of) the quadratic variation — a two-sided refinement of Doob's inequality.
50 Representation & BDG
Doob's \(L^2\)-maximal inequality bounds \(E\big[\sup_{s\le t}M_s^2\big]\) by:
\(E[\sup_{s\le t}M_s^2]\le 4E[M_t^2]\) (the \(p=2\) case, constant \((p/(p-1))^p=4\)). This is precisely the bound used to control \(\sup_u B_{T\wedge u}^2\) in 5.2(b) and the \(L^2\)-continuity argument in 4.1(d).
