01 Optional sampling & Wald
Let \(T\) be a bounded stopping time. Compute \(E[B_T]\).
Since \(T\wedge t=T\) is a bounded stopping time and \(B\) is a martingale, the optional sampling theorem gives \(E[B_T\mid\mathcal F_0]=B_0=0\); taking expectations, \(E[B_T]=0\) (5.2(a)).
02 Optional sampling & Wald
Let \(T\) be a bounded stopping time with \(E[T]=9\). Compute \(E[B_T^2]\).
\(B_t^2-t\) is a martingale, so optional sampling gives \(E[B_T^2-T]=0\), i.e. \(E[B_T^2]=E[T]=9\) — the Wald second identity (5.2(a),(c)).
03 Optional sampling & Wald
For bounded stopping times \(S,T\), the identity for \(E[(B_T-B_S)^2]\) is:
Expanding \(E[(B_T-B_S)^2]=E[B_T^2]-2E[B_SB_T]+E[B_S^2]=E[T]-2E[S\wedge T]+E[S]=E[\,|T-S|\,]\), using \(a+b-2(a\wedge b)=|a-b|\) (5.2(f)).
04 Optional sampling & Wald
For (suitable) stopping times \(S,T\), the cross term \(E[B_SB_T]\) equals:
Using \(E[B_T\mid\mathcal F_{S\wedge T}]=B_{S\wedge T}\) and splitting on \(\{S\le T\}\) and its complement, \(E[B_SB_T]=E[B_{S\wedge T}^2]=E[S\wedge T]\) (5.2(e)).
05 Optional sampling & Wald
An adapted, integrable process \(X\) is a martingale if and only if \(E[X_T]=E[X_0]\) for:
"\(\Rightarrow\)" is optional sampling. For "\(\Leftarrow\)", take \(T=s\mathbf 1_A+t\mathbf 1_{A^c}\) with \(A\in\mathcal F_s\); \(E[X_T]=E[X_0]=E[X_t]\) forces \(E[X_s\mathbf 1_A]=E[X_t\mathbf 1_A]\), the martingale property (5.1(b)).

08 Itô integrals
Evaluate the Itô integral \(\displaystyle\int_0^t B_s\,\mathrm dB_s\).
From \(x(y-x)=\tfrac12\big(y^2-x^2-(y-x)^2\big)\), the Riemann–Itô sum telescopes to \(\tfrac12\big(B_t^2-\sum(\Delta B)^2\big)\to\tfrac12(B_t^2-t)\) in probability. The extra \(-t\) is the Itô correction absent in ordinary calculus (5.4(e)).
09 Itô integrals
Compute \(\operatorname{var}\!\Big(\displaystyle\int_0^t B_s\,\mathrm dB_s\Big)\).
The integral is a mean-zero martingale, so its variance is the expected quadratic variation \(E\big[\int_0^t B_s^2\,\mathrm ds\big]=\int_0^t s\,\mathrm ds=t^2/2\) (5.4(d)).
10 Itô integrals
The quadratic variation \(\big\langle\int B\,\mathrm dB,\int B\,\mathrm dB\big\rangle_t\) equals:
For a stochastic integral \(\big\langle\int H\,\mathrm dB\big\rangle_t=\int_0^t H_s^2\,\mathrm d\langle B,B\rangle_s\). With \(H_s=B_s\) and \(\mathrm d\langle B,B\rangle_s=\mathrm ds\), this is \(\int_0^t B_s^2\,\mathrm ds\) (5.4(b)).
11 Itô integrals
Compute \(E\Big[\displaystyle\int_0^t B_s^2\,\mathrm ds\Big]\).
By Fubini, \(E\big[\int_0^t B_s^2\,\mathrm ds\big]=\int_0^t E[B_s^2]\,\mathrm ds=\int_0^t s\,\mathrm ds=t^2/2\) (5.4(c)).
12 Itô integrals
Is \(\big(e^{\lambda B_t}\big)_{t\ge0}\) a martingale for \(\lambda\neq0\)?
A martingale has constant expectation, but \(E[e^{\lambda B_t}]=e^{\lambda^2t/2}\) grows unless \(\lambda=0\). The fix is the compensator: \(e^{\lambda B_t-\lambda^2t/2}\) is a martingale (5.3(a)).
13 Itô integrals
Which of the following is a (square-integrable) martingale?
\(\int_0^t e^{-s}\,\mathrm dB_s\) has a deterministic integrand with \(\int_0^t e^{-2s}\,\mathrm ds<\infty\), so it is a square-integrable martingale. The pre-factor \(e^{-t}\) in \(Y_t\) destroys the martingale property (5.3(c)(i)).
14 Itô integrals
Why is \(Y_t=e^{-t}\displaystyle\int_0^t e^{s}\,\mathrm dB_s\) not a martingale?
Writing \(M_t=\int_0^t e^{s}\,\mathrm dB_s\) (a martingale) and \(Y_t=e^{-t}M_t\): \(E[Y_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=e^{-t}M_s=e^{-(t-s)}Y_s\neq Y_s\). This is the mean-reversion of an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process (5.3(c)(ii)).
15 Itô isometry
For deterministic \(f\), the Itô isometry gives \(\operatorname{var}\!\Big(\displaystyle\int_0^t f(s)\,\mathrm dB_s\Big)=\):
The integral is a centred martingale, so \(\operatorname{var}=E\big[(\int_0^t f\,\mathrm dB)^2\big]=E\big[\int_0^t f(s)^2\,\mathrm ds\big]=\int_0^t f(s)^2\,\mathrm ds\) (Itô isometry, 5.3(c),(d)).
16 Itô isometry
Compute \(\operatorname{var}\!\Big(\displaystyle\int_0^t e^{-s}\,\mathrm dB_s\Big)\).
By the isometry, \(\int_0^t (e^{-s})^2\,\mathrm ds=\int_0^t e^{-2s}\,\mathrm ds=\tfrac12(1-e^{-2t})\). Note it stays bounded (by \(\tfrac12\)) as \(t\to\infty\), so the integral is \(L^2\)-bounded (5.3(c)(i)).
17 Itô isometry
For \(X_t=\displaystyle\int_0^t f(s)\big(\sin B_s+\cos B_s\big)\,\mathrm dB_s\), the variance \(\operatorname{var}(X_t)\) equals:
By the isometry, \(\operatorname{var}(X_t)=\int_0^t f(s)^2\,E\big[(\sin B_s+\cos B_s)^2\big]\,\mathrm ds\), and \(E[(\sin B_s+\cos B_s)^2]=E[1+\sin(2B_s)]=1\), leaving \(\int_0^t f(s)^2\,\mathrm ds\) (5.3(d)(iii)).
18 Itô isometry
The simplification \(E\big[(\sin B_s+\cos B_s)^2\big]=1\) relies on:
\((\sin x+\cos x)^2=1+\sin(2x)\). Since \(B_s\) is symmetric and \(\sin\) is odd, \(E[\sin(2B_s)]=0\), so the expectation is exactly \(1\) (5.3(d)(ii),(iii)).
19 Itô isometry
Compute \(E[\cos B_t]\) for standard Brownian motion.
Take the real part of the characteristic function: \(E[e^{iB_t}]=e^{-t/2}\), so \(E[\cos B_t]=\operatorname{Re}\,e^{-t/2}=e^{-t/2}\) (while \(E[\sin B_t]=0\) by symmetry, cf. 5.3(d)(ii)).
20 Stratonovich & α-rule
Evaluating \(\int B\,\mathrm dB\) with the integrand sampled at the intermediate point \(\tilde t_{i-1}=t_{i-1}+\alpha\,\Delta t_i\), the sum converges to:
The evaluation point adds a correction \(\alpha t\) in \(L^2\): the limit is \(\tfrac12(B_t^2-t)+\alpha t=\tfrac12\big(B_t^2+(2\alpha-1)t\big)\). The evaluation point, not just the mesh, changes the answer (5.4(f)).
21 Stratonovich & α-rule
The Stratonovich integral (midpoint, \(\alpha=\tfrac12\)) \(\displaystyle\int_0^t B_s\circ\mathrm dB_s\) equals:
Set \(\alpha=\tfrac12\) in \(\tfrac12(B_t^2+(2\alpha-1)t)\): the \(t\) terms cancel, giving \(\tfrac12 B_t^2\). The Stratonovich integral obeys the ordinary chain rule — no Itô correction (5.4(f)).
22 Stratonovich & α-rule
The limiting process \(L^{(\alpha)}_t=\tfrac12\big(B_t^2+(2\alpha-1)t\big)\) is a martingale if and only if:
\(L^{(\alpha)}_t=\tfrac12(B_t^2-t)+\alpha t\): a martingale plus the drift \(\alpha t\). A martingale started at \(0\) can have no non-constant finite-variation drift, so \(\alpha=0\) (the Itô case) (5.4(g)).
23 Stratonovich & α-rule
Using the right endpoint (\(\alpha=1\)) to sample the integrand gives, for \(\int B\,\mathrm dB\), the limit:
Set \(\alpha=1\): \(\tfrac12(B_t^2+(2\cdot1-1)t)=\tfrac12(B_t^2+t)\). Sampling ahead of the increment adds a positive drift, mirror-image of the Itô correction (5.4(f)).
24 Local martingales
A continuous local martingale \(M\) with \(M_0=0\) that also has paths of finite variation must be:
Theorem 4.8: a continuous local martingale of finite variation with \(M_0=0\) is the zero process. Its quadratic variation vanishes, forcing \(M\equiv0\) (Slides wk 3.2).
25 Local martingales
Every continuous martingale is:
Take the deterministic reducing sequence \(T_n=n\); each stopped process \((M_{t\wedge n})\) is a UI martingale (Theorem 3.21). So every continuous martingale is a continuous local martingale (Slides wk 4.1).
26 Local martingales
The canonical localizing sequence reducing a continuous local martingale with \(M_0=0\) is:
\(T_n=\inf\{t:|M_t|\ge n\}\) is a hitting time of the closed set \([n,\infty)\); the stopped process \(M^{T_n}\) is bounded by \(n\), hence a UI martingale (Prop 4.7(iii)).
27 Local martingales
A nonnegative continuous local martingale \(M\) with \(E[|M_0|]<\infty\) is always a:
By Fatou for conditional expectations, \(E[M_t\mid\mathcal F_s]\le\liminf_n E[M^{T_n}_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=M_s\): a supermartingale. It need not be a true martingale (Prop 4.7(i)).
28 Local martingales
A continuous local martingale \(M\) with \(|M_t|\le Z\) for an integrable \(Z\) is a:
Domination \(|M^{T_n}_t|\le Z\) lets the dominated convergence theorem pass the limit inside: \(E[M_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=\lim_n M^{T_n}_s=M_s\), a UI martingale (Prop 4.7(ii)).
29 Local martingales
The key tool proving "nonnegative continuous local martingale \(\Rightarrow\) supermartingale" is:
Positivity lets conditional Fatou apply to \(M_t=\liminf_n M^{T_n}_t\), giving \(E[M_t\mid\mathcal F_s]\le M_s\) (Proof sketch, Prop 4.7(i)).
30 Bracket process
For two independent Brownian motions \(B^{(1)},B^{(2)}\), the bracket \(\langle B^{(1)},B^{(2)}\rangle_t\) equals:
Proposition 4.16: independent Brownian motions have identically-zero bracket, \(\langle B^{(1)},B^{(2)}\rangle_t=0\), since \(B^{(1)}_tB^{(2)}_t\) is itself a martingale (Slides wk 4.1).
31 Bracket process
The polarisation identity expresses the bracket \(\langle X,Y\rangle_t\) as:
Exactly as for an inner product: \(\langle X,Y\rangle=\tfrac12(\langle X{+}Y,X{+}Y\rangle-\langle X,X\rangle-\langle Y,Y\rangle)\), from the bilinearity of the covariation (Prop 4.15).
32 Bracket process
As a map on pairs of processes, \((X,Y)\mapsto\langle X,Y\rangle\) is:
The covariation is symmetric and bilinear (Prop 4.15), which is precisely what makes the polarisation identity valid (Slides wk 4.1).
33 Bracket process
For a continuous finite-variation process \(A\) and a continuous local martingale \(M\), \(\langle A,M\rangle_t\) equals:
Proposition 4.21: the covariation of a finite-variation process with anything is zero, \(\langle A,M\rangle_t=0\) — the "smooth" part registers no covariation (Slides wk 4.1).
34 Bracket process
For \(M,\tilde M\in\mathbb H^2\) (\(L^2\)-bounded martingales from \(0\)), the Itô isometry for martingales reads \(E[M_\infty\tilde M_\infty]=\):
The \(\mathbb H^2\) inner product via terminal values equals the expected bracket: \((M,\tilde M)_{\mathbb H^2}=E[M_\infty\tilde M_\infty]=E[\langle M,\tilde M\rangle_\infty]\) (Prop 4.15(v), Slides wk 4.1).
35 Bracket process
The bracket \(\langle M,\tilde M\rangle\) is characterised as the unique finite-variation process (starting at \(0\)) making which process a local martingale?
Proposition 4.15(i),(iii): \(\langle M,\tilde M\rangle\) is the unique continuous finite-variation process, zero at \(0\), such that \(M_t\tilde M_t-\langle M,\tilde M\rangle_t\) is a continuous local martingale.
36 Semimartingales & QV
For a continuous semimartingale \(X=M+A\) (\(M\) local martingale, \(A\) finite variation), the quadratic variation \(\langle X,X\rangle\) equals:
By bilinearity, \(\langle X,X\rangle=\langle M,M\rangle+2\langle M,A\rangle+\langle A,A\rangle\), but \(\langle A,A\rangle=0\) and \(\langle M,A\rangle=0\). So the quadratic variation is entirely that of the martingale part, \(\langle M,M\rangle\) (Def 4.20, Slides wk 4.1).
37 Semimartingales & QV
Let \(X_t=B_t+3t\). Compute \(\langle X,X\rangle_t\).
The drift \(3t\) is finite variation and contributes nothing; only the Brownian part counts: \(\langle X,X\rangle_t=\langle B,B\rangle_t=t\) (Def 4.20).
38 Semimartingales & QV
A continuous process of finite variation has quadratic variation:
Proposition 4.21: \(\langle A,A\rangle_t=0\). A finite-variation path is too "smooth" to accumulate squared increments — unlike a Brownian path (Slides wk 4.1).
39 Semimartingales & QV
The canonical decomposition \(X=M+A\) of a continuous semimartingale (with \(M\) a local martingale and \(A\) finite variation, \(A_0=0\)) is:
Definition 4.4: the decomposition is unique. Two decompositions would differ by a continuous finite-variation local martingale from \(0\), which is \(0\) by Theorem 4.8 (Slides wk 4.1).

42 Integrated BM
The increments of integrated Brownian motion \(I_t=\int_0^t B_s\,\mathrm ds\) are:
\(\operatorname{var}\big(\int_s^{s+t}B_r\,\mathrm dr\big)=st^2+t^3/3\) depends on \(s\), so increments are not stationary; and e.g. \(\operatorname{cov}(I_1,I_2-I_1)=1/2\neq0\), so not independent (4.3(h)).

44 Gaussian martingales
Let \(M\) be a centred Gaussian martingale with \(\Gamma(t,t)=E[M_t^2]\). Which process is a martingale?
Since \(M_t-M_s\) is centred Gaussian with variance \(\Gamma(t,t)-\Gamma(s,s)\), its MGF cancels the compensator: \(\exp(\theta M_t-\tfrac12\theta^2\Gamma(t,t))\) is a martingale (4.1(i)).
45 Gaussian martingales
Suppose \(\Gamma(t,t)=2t\). For which \(\theta>0\) is \(\exp(\theta M_t-t)\) a martingale?
Match \(\tfrac12\theta^2\Gamma(t,t)=t\): \(\tfrac12\theta^2(2t)=t\Rightarrow\theta^2=1\Rightarrow\theta=1\) (4.2(b) style).
46 Gaussian martingales
For a Gaussian martingale with \(\Gamma(t,t)=2t\), the quadratic variation \(\langle M,M\rangle_t\) equals:
For a Gaussian martingale, \(M_t^2-\Gamma(t,t)\) is a martingale, so by uniqueness \(\langle M,M\rangle_t=\Gamma(t,t)=2t\) (4.1(h), 4.2(a)).
47 Gaussian martingales
For a \(d\)-dimensional Brownian motion, \(|B_t|^2-dt\) is a martingale. For \(d=3\) this reads:
\(|B_t|^2-dt=\sum_{i=1}^d\big((B^{(i)}_t)^2-t\big)\) is a sum of \(d\) independent martingales. For \(d=3\): \(|B_t|^2-3t\) (3.2(b)).

49 Bridge & correlated
With \(W^{(1)}_t=\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,B^{(1)}_t+\rho B^{(2)}_t\) for independent BMs, the process \(W^{(1)}_tB^{(2)}_t-\rho t\) is:
Expand \(W^{(1)}_tB^{(2)}_t=\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,B^{(1)}_tB^{(2)}_t+\rho\big((B^{(2)}_t)^2\big)\); using that \(B^{(1)}B^{(2)}\) and \((B^{(2)})^2-t\) are martingales, \(W^{(1)}_tB^{(2)}_t-\rho t\) is a martingale (3.3(c)).
50 Bridge & correlated
For the same construction \(W^{(1)}_t=\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,B^{(1)}_t+\rho B^{(2)}_t\), the correlation \(\operatorname{corr}(W^{(1)}_t,B^{(2)}_t)\) is:
\(\operatorname{cov}(W^{(1)}_t,B^{(2)}_t)=\rho t\) and both have variance \(t\), so \(\operatorname{corr}=\rho t/(\sqrt t\sqrt t)=\rho\). The construction builds a BM with prescribed correlation \(\rho\) to \(B^{(2)}\) (3.3(b)).
