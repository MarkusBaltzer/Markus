00 Orientation

How to read this

The course has a single spine. You observe iid risks \(Z_1,\dots,Z_n\) (claim sizes, say), you build a statistic, and you want two things: consistency (it lands on the truth) and a limit law (usually Gaussian, so you can put error bars — Solvency II capital, confidence intervals). Chapter 1 does this first for numbers, then for whole curves (the empirical distribution function as a random path). Chapter 2 applies the same reflexes to smoothers that borrow strength from covariates.

Four moves recur so often they are worth naming now, because almost every proof is a recombination of them:

Mental model

Think of an estimator as a spring settling. The LLN says where it settles (the mean position). The CLT describes the jitter around that point — and the width of that jitter, divided by \(\sqrt n\), is your confidence interval. The functional versions in the second half say the same thing about a spring at every point of a curve simultaneously.

The prerequisite toolkit

This course generalises three things you're assumed to already own: the classical limit theorems, integration against a distribution, and the standard families of loss distributions. Gamma facts to keep loaded

\(\Gamma(\gamma+1)=\gamma\,\Gamma(\gamma)\) and \(\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!\); and the identity the compound-Poisson problem hinges on — a sum of \(n\) independent \(\Gamma(\gamma,c)\) variables is \(\Gamma(n\gamma,c)\).

Expectation machinery

  • Indicators do the heavy lifting: \(\mathbb E[\mathbf 1_A]=\mathbb P(A)\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf 1_A,\mathbf 1_B)=\mathbb P(A\cap B)-\mathbb P(A)\mathbb P(B)\) — the single most-used line on the exam.
  • Tower property: \(\mathbb E[X]=\mathbb E[\mathbb E[X\mid Y]]\), with the companion law of total variance \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)]+\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb E[X\mid Y])\).
  • Compound sums (Wald): for \(S=\sum_{i=1}^N Y_i\) with \(N\) independent of \((Y_i)\), \(\mathbb E[S]=\mathbb E[N]\,\mathbb E[Y]\); in the compound-Poisson case \(\operatorname{Var}(S)=\lambda\,\mathbb E[Y^2]\).
  • MGF & heavy tails: \(M(t)=\mathbb E[e^{tX}]\); a law is heavy-tailed precisely when \(M(t)=\infty\) for every \(t\gt0\).

Integrating against a distribution

The empirical CDF \(F^{(n)}\) is itself a (random) distribution function, so you integrate against one constantly. Be fluent with the Riemann–Stieltjes / Lebesgue integral \(\int\varphi\,dF\): against a jump measure it is a sum \(\sum_k\varphi(x_k)\,\Delta F(x_k)\); against a density it is \(\int\varphi(x)f(x)\,dx\).

The analysis moves the proofs reuse
  • Integration by parts on tails: \(\int_u^\infty\bar F\) is the backbone of every mean-excess computation.
  • Dominated / monotone convergence to exchange \(\lim\) and \(\int\) — the engine behind Breiman's lemma and the Kaplan–Meier-integral consistency.
  • First-order Taylor: \(\log(1-x)\approx-x\) and \((1+x)^a\approx1+ax\) for small \(x\) — used verbatim in the Hadamard-derivative and regular-variation arguments.
  • Asymptotic ratios (L'Hôpital) and Mill's ratio \(\Phi(z)\sim-\varphi(z)/z\) as \(z\to-\infty\), for the lognormal tail.

Landau notation — deterministic and stochastic

Keep both families straight; the course uses them side by side. Deterministically, \(f=o(g)\) means \(f/g\to0\) and \(f=O(g)\) means \(f/g\) stays bounded. Stochastically, \(X_n=o_P(1)\) means \(X_n\xrightarrow{P}0\), while \(X_n=O_P(1)\) means the sequence is tight (bounded in probability). The bridge between them — "\(o_P(1)\) apart \(\Rightarrow\) same weak limit" — is the very first lemma of Week 1.

The classical limit theorems this course upgrades

  • LLN: \(\tfrac1n\sum_i g(Z_i)\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mathbb E[g(Z_1)]\) — the source of every consistency proof.
  • CLT (Lindeberg / Lyapounov): \(\sqrt n(\bar Z_n-\mu)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\sigma^2)\) — the source of every error bar. Chapter 1 replaces the number \(\bar Z_n\) by the whole curve \(F^{(n)}\), and the Gaussian by the Brownian bridge.

Linear algebra & the multivariate normal

You need covariance matrices \(\Sigma\), quadratic forms, and the fact that a Gaussian vector or process is completely determined by its mean and covariance. Two tools recur throughout. The Cramér–Wold device — a random vector converges in law iff every linear combination \(a^\top X_n\) does — reduces the multivariate CLT to the univariate one. And the Brownian bridge \(B(t)=W(t)-tW(1)\), pinned at \(B(0)=B(1)=0\) with

\[\operatorname{Cov}(B(s),B(t))=s\wedge t-st,\]

is the exact object the empirical process converges to. Recognise this covariance and you recognise a bridge.

Statistics & processes vocabulary

  • Estimator language: bias, consistency \((\hat\theta_n\xrightarrow{P}\theta)\), asymptotic normality, and the empirical distribution function \(F^{(n)}(t)=\tfrac1n\sum_i\mathbf 1_{Z_i\le t}\).
  • MLE & exponential families: the likelihood/score picture, and that a one-parameter exponential family writes \(f=\exp\{(x\theta-b(\theta))/\phi+c(x,\phi)\}\) with mean identity \(b'(\theta)=\mu\). Problem 1 is exactly this.
  • Poisson & jump processes: the Poisson process behind compound claims, and finite-state Markov / jump processes with transition probabilities \(P_{ij}(s,t)\) — the objects of Problem 3.

Counting processes & at-risk sets

A jump process is bookkept by counts. Write \(N_{jk}(t)=\#\{s\le t:Z_{s-}=j,Z_s=k\}\) for the number of \(j\to k\) transitions, and \(Y_j(t)\) for the number at risk in state \(j\) just before \(t\). Two facts do all the work:

  • Occupation as a transition balance. The state indicator decomposes as \(\mathbf 1_{Z_t=j}=\mathbf 1_{Z_0=j}+\sum_{k\ne j}\big(N_{kj}(t)-N_{jk}(t)\big)\) — arrivals minus departures. This is the identity (Prop. 12.3 in the exam script) that turns the occupation estimator into an average of counting processes, which is why the LLN and abstract Glivenko–Cantelli then apply to it.
  • Nelson–Aalen increment. \(d\hat A_{jk}(s)=dN_{jk}(s)/Y_j(s)\) estimates the transition intensity from exactly the subjects still observed — the mechanism that makes censoring harmless.

Product-limit and product integral

Both survival estimators are products, not sums, and you must be fluent reading them.

  • Kaplan–Meier (survival form): \(1-F^{(n)}(t)=\prod_{W_{(i)}\le t}\big(1-\tfrac{\delta_{(i)}}{n-i+1}\big)\). Its jump at an uncensored time is read off with the telescoping identity \(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(1-\tfrac1{n-j+1})=\tfrac{n-k+1}{n}\) — the exact move that gives the \(1/n\) jump (and \(2/n\) under a tie) in Problem 2.2.
  • Aalen–Johansen (multistate): the transition matrix is the product integral \(\hat P(0,t)=\prod_{(0,t]}\big(\mathrm{Id}+d\hat A(s)\big)\), and occupation is its first row, \(\hat p_j(t)=\sum_i\hat p_i(0)\hat P_{ij}(0,t)\).

Empirical-process vocabulary: brackets, entropy, Donsker

Problem 3.4 and all of Week 2 speak this language. For a class of functions \(\mathcal F\):

  • A bracket \([l,u]\) is the set of \(f\) with \(l\le f\le u\); its size is the \(L^2(P)\) distance \(\|u-l\|_{L^2}\).
  • The bracketing number \(N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L^2)\) is how many \(\varepsilon\)-brackets cover \(\mathcal F\); the entropy integral is \(\int_0^1\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)}\,d\varepsilon\).
  • Two thresholds: a finite bracketing number at every \(\varepsilon\) makes \(\mathcal F\) Glivenko–Cantelli (uniform LLN); a convergent entropy integral makes it Donsker (uniform CLT / tightness). That is the whole logic separating a consistency proof from a weak-convergence proof.
Càdlàg, BV, monotone — why they matter

Right-continuous-with-left-limits (càdlàg), bounded-variation, non-decreasing functions are the well-behaved objects the theorems accept. In Problem 3.3 the counting processes \(N_{jk}\) are bounded, non-decreasing and càdlàg — that trio is exactly the hypothesis of the abstract Glivenko–Cantelli theorem, so uniform consistency follows for free once pointwise consistency (LLN) is in hand.

Reading the exponential dispersion family off the page

Membership proofs are pattern-matching against \(f(x;\theta,\phi)=\exp\{(x\theta-b(\theta))/\phi+c(x,\phi)\}\). The drill: isolate the term linear in \(x\) to read \(\theta/\phi\); collect the remaining \(\theta\)-dependent constant as \(-b(\theta)/\phi\); dump everything else into \(c\). Then two derivatives give you the moments,

\[\mathbb E[X]=b'(\theta),\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\phi\,b''(\theta)=\phi\,V(\mu),\]

where \(V(\mu)=b''(\theta)\) is the variance function — \(V(\mu)=\mu\) for Poisson, \(\mu^2\) for gamma, and \(\mu^p\) for the whole Tweedie family. Verifying \(b'(\theta)=\mu\) is the certificate the exam rewards.

01 Foundations

Three ways a sequence can converge

For \(\mathbb{R}^p\)-valued \(Z_n\) there are three modes, from strongest to weakest, and knowing which one a theorem gives you tells you what you're allowed to conclude.

  • Almost sure \(\;Z_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}Z\): with probability one the whole trajectory eventually gets and stays close. This is convergence of paths.
  • In probability \(\;Z_n\xrightarrow{P}Z\): for any tolerance \(\varepsilon\), the chance of being further than \(\varepsilon\) away vanishes. Individual excursions are allowed forever, they just get rare.
  • In distribution \(\;Z_n\xrightarrow{d}Z\): only the law settles — \(\mathbb{E}f(Z_n)\to\mathbb{E}f(Z)\) for bounded continuous \(f\). The \(Z_n\) needn't even live near \(Z\); a symmetric limit can't tell \(+Z\) from \(-Z\).
Lemma 1.1 · the ladder

\(a.s.\Rightarrow P\Rightarrow d\). The reverse implications fail in general — with one exception: convergence to a constant \(c\) is the same in probability and in distribution. That exception is the engine of Slutsky's lemma below.

02 Foundations

Stochastic bookkeeping: \(o_P\) and \(O_P\)

This is just sloppiness with a licence. \(Z_n=O_P(1)\) means "bounded in probability" — the mass doesn't run off to infinity. \(Z_n=o_P(1)\) means \(Z_n\xrightarrow{P}0\). Rates attach by division: \(Z_n=O_P(r_n)\) iff \(Z_n/r_n=O_P(1)\).

Why you care

Two facts do almost all the work. First (Example 1.1): convergence in distribution implies \(O_P(1)\) — a settling law can't leak mass to infinity. Second, remainder terms in Taylor expansions get absorbed: if the leading term is \(O_P(a_n^{-1})\) and the remainder is \(o_P\) of the increment, the remainder is \(o_P(a_n^{-1})\) and simply drops out after you multiply by \(a_n\). That is the entire delta-method proof in one sentence.

Example 1.2, in words

If \(Z_n=O_P(a_n)\) and \(W_n=o_P(\|Z_n\|)\), then \(W_n=o_P(a_n)\). Read it as: "a small fraction of a bounded thing is small." You split the event \(\{\|W_n\|/a_n\ge\varepsilon\}\) using an intermediate scale \(\varepsilon_1\) — one piece controls \(W_n\) relative to \(Z_n\), the other controls \(Z_n\) relative to \(a_n\) — and both go to zero.

03 Foundations

Divide and conquer: continuous mapping & Slutsky

These two let you break a messy statistic into a "converges to something random" part and a "converges to a constant" part, handle them separately, and glue back.

Continuous mapping (Thm 1.2)

If \(Z_n\to Z\) (in any of the three modes) and \(g\) is continuous, then \(g(Z_n)\to g(Z)\) in the same mode. You may apply logs, ratios, maxima — anything continuous — without losing convergence.

Slutsky (Lemma 1.3)

If \(Z_n\xrightarrow{d}Z\) and \(W_n\xrightarrow{d}c\) (a constant), then \((Z_n,W_n)\xrightarrow{d}(Z,c)\). Hence \(Z_n+W_n\xrightarrow{d}Z+c\) and \(W_nZ_n\xrightarrow{d}cZ\).

Trap

Slutsky needs the second sequence to converge to a constant. Two genuinely random sequences converging in distribution do not automatically converge jointly — their dependence is unknown. This is exactly why the empirical-process chapter has to work so hard to get joint (pathwise) statements rather than pointwise ones.

04 Foundations

The central limit theorems

Three flavours, matched to three data situations.

Laplace — iid, the default

\(\sqrt n\big(\tfrac1n\sum Z_i-\mathbb{E}Z_1\big)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\operatorname{Var}(Z_1))\), given a finite second moment. This is the one you reach for 90% of the time.

Lindeberg — triangular arrays, non-identical rows

Real portfolios aren't identically distributed: different policies, different exposures. The triangular array \(Z_{nm}\) (row \(n\) has \(m=1,\dots,n\) independent entries) handles this. If the rows are centred, their variances sum to \(\Sigma\), and Lindeberg's condition holds — no single term dominates the sum —

then \(\sum_m Z_{nm}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\Sigma)\).

Lindeberg in plain terms

The condition says: the total variance contributed by the "unusually large" summands (those exceeding \(c\)) vanishes. No one policy is allowed to carry the portfolio. Lyapounov's condition (a bounded \((2{+}\delta)\)-moment sum \(\to 0\)) is a easier-to-check sufficient version — this is the one you verify in practice, and it's exactly the step used in every kernel-CLT proof of Chapter 2.

Why it's not just continuous mapping

Continuous mapping transforms after the limit is taken. The delta method transforms the raw estimator and asks how the transformation reshapes the fluctuations. A smooth \(h\) looks linear up close, so it multiplies the Gaussian noise by the local slope \(\nabla h(c)\). The whole proof is: Taylor-expand, note the remainder is \(o_P(a_n^{-1})\) (from §02), multiply by \(a_n\), let it drop out.

This single lemma powers most of the "exploratory tools" below — the trick each time is to write the estimator as \(h(T_n)\) for a vector average \(T_n\), get \(T_n\)'s joint CLT from Laplace, then read off the sandwich variance \(\nabla h^\top \Sigma\, \nabla h\).

08 Exploratory risk tools

The mean excess function — a tail thermometer

\(e_F(u)=\mathbb E[Z-u\mid Z>u]\): given a claim already exceeds \(u\), how much more do you expect to pay? Its shape is the tail's fingerprint:

  • Flat \(\Rightarrow\) exponential tail (memoryless: exceedance size doesn't grow with \(u\)).
  • Increasing / linear \(\Rightarrow\) heavy (Pareto) tail — \(e_F(u)=u/(\alpha-1)\), the worse the tail the steeper.
  • Decreasing \(\Rightarrow\) light (sub-exponential from below) tail.

The empirical version \(e_n(u)\) is asymptotically Gaussian at each fixed \(u\) (Thm 1.12), with variance \(\big(e_F^{(2)}(u)-e_F(u)^2\big)/(1-F(u))\) — note it blows up as \(u\) enters the sparse tail, which is the honest statement that far-tail estimation is hard.

10 From points to paths

Weak convergence of processes

Now the object is a random function — \(t\mapsto \sqrt n\,(F^{(n)}(t)-F(t))\) lives in a space of càdlàg paths. Two new problems appear, and the chapter's abstractions exist only to solve them.

Problem 1: finite-dimensional laws aren't enough

Knowing the joint law at every finite grid \(t_1,\dots,t_m\) does not determine pathwise behaviour. A test like "does \(F\) match this parametric family?" depends on \(\sup_t\) — the maximum gap can occur between any two grid points, and the probability it lands exactly on a pre-chosen grid is zero. You need convergence of the whole path.

Problem 2: measurability breaks under the sup-norm

The natural metric on càdlàg space is the uniform norm, but then the empirical path isn't Borel-measurable. The fix is elegant: replace \(\mathbb E\) by the outer expectation \(\mathbb E^\ast\) (infimum over measurable majorants). It agrees with \(\mathbb E\) when there's no problem, and lets every theorem go through when there is.

Weak convergence (Def 1.8) + the toolkit

\(X_n\xrightarrow{d}X\) means \(\mathbb E^\ast f(X_n)\to\mathbb E f(X)\) for bounded continuous \(f\). The finite-dimensional generalisations of everything survive: an outer-probability continuous mapping theorem (1.18), a general Slutsky (1.19), a marginal-to-joint lemma (1.17) that needs tightness (mass stays on compacts), and a monotone Dini theorem (1.20) that is Glivenko–Cantelli's twin. You rarely touch \(\mathbb E^\ast\) directly — you cite these.

12 From points to paths

Donsker classes & bracketing entropy

Index the empirical process by a class of functions \(\mathcal F\): \(\mathbb G^{(n)}f=\sqrt n(\mathbb P^{(n)}f-\mathbb P f)\). Two questions:

  • Glivenko–Cantelli class: does \(\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|\mathbb P^{(n)}f-\mathbb P f|\to0\)? (uniform LLN over \(\mathcal F\))
  • Donsker class: does \(\mathbb G^{(n)}\xrightarrow{d}\mathbb G\), a tight Gaussian process? (uniform CLT over \(\mathcal F\))

The answer is controlled by how "wiggly" \(\mathcal F\) is, measured by bracketing numbers \(N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|)\): the fewest \(\varepsilon\)-thick brackets \([l,u]\) needed to sandwich every \(f\). Small entropy = tame class = limit theorems hold.

The two entropy theorems

GC (1.27): finite \(N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_1)\) for every \(\varepsilon\) \(\Rightarrow\) Glivenko–Cantelli.
Donsker (1.28): a finite bracketing integral \(\int_0^1\!\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2)}\,d\varepsilon\) \(\Rightarrow\) Donsker. Theorem 1.29 is the moving-class version needed when the functions themselves depend on \(n\) — exactly the kernel-smoothing situation of Ch. 2.

The canonical calculation

For the indicator class \(\mathcal F=\{\mathbf 1_{[0,t]}:t\}\), take brackets \([\mathbf 1_{[0,t_{j-1})},\mathbf 1_{[0,t_j)}]\) at grid points where \(F\) rises by \(<\varepsilon^2\). Then \(N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2)\lesssim 2/\varepsilon^2\), the entropy integral converges, and \(\mathcal F\) is Donsker. This one computation — done in Thm 1.30 and re-run for the bivariate and conditional cases in the exercises — is the reason the empirical CDF converges to a Brownian bridge.

14 From points to paths

Quantiles, Value-at-Risk, and copulas

VaR at level \(\alpha\) is simply the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the loss: \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(Z)=F^{-1}(\alpha)\). Solvency II's "200-year event" is \(\mathrm{VaR}_{0.995}\). As a risk measure VaR is monotone, translation-equivariant, homogeneous, and subadditive for elliptical losses (the caveat that launched coherent risk measures).

Quantile process (Thm 1.31)

Feed the bridge result through the Hadamard-differentiable inverse map: \(\sqrt n\big([F^{(n)}]^{-1}-F^{-1}\big)\xrightarrow{d}-\dfrac{B\circ F}{f\circ F^{-1}}\) on \([p,q]\). The density \(f\) in the denominator is why sparse tails give wide quantile error bars — small \(f\) inflates the limit. For very high quantiles, switch to the extreme-value tools of Ch. 3.

A copula strips out the marginals and keeps only the dependence: by Sklar, \(H(x_1,\dots,x_r)=C(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_r(x_r))\) with \(C\) a distribution on \([0,1]^r\) with uniform margins. The empirical copula process \(\sqrt n(C^{(n)}-C)\) is again a functional-delta image of the bridge (Thm 1.32) — the notes assemble it from four stacked Hadamard-differentiable maps, a clean showcase of the chain rule.

Actuarial reading

Copulas let you couple, say, motor and household claims that spike together in a storm while modelling each margin separately. The empirical copula gives a distribution-free first estimate; its Gaussian limit gives the uncertainty band.

Problem 1 — Compound Poisson–Gamma & the exponential dispersion family

\(N\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)\), \(Y_i\stackrel{iid}\sim\Gamma(\gamma,c)\) (shape \(\gamma\), rate \(c\)) independent of \(N\), and \(S=\sum_{i=1}^N Y_i\).

1.1Point mass and density of \(S\)›

Point mass. \(S=0\) exactly when there are no claims, so \(\;\mathbb P(S=0)=\mathbb P(N=0)=e^{-\lambda}.\)

Density on \((0,\infty)\). Given \(N=n\ge1\), \(S=\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\) is a sum of \(n\) independent \(\Gamma(\gamma,c)\) variables, hence \(\Gamma(n\gamma,c)\) with density \(f_n(x)=\dfrac{c^{n\gamma}x^{n\gamma-1}e^{-cx}}{\Gamma(n\gamma)}\). Mixing over \(N\) with the hint,

This is the density of a Tweedie / compound-Poisson–gamma law: an atom at 0 plus a continuous, right-skewed positive part.

1.2Membership in the exponential dispersion family›

The EDF form is \(f(x;\theta,\phi)=\exp\!\big\{\tfrac{x\theta-b(\theta)}{\phi}+c(x,\phi)\big\}\). The clean, high-value way to "show" the claimed parametrisation is correct is to verify the mean identity \(b'(\theta)=\mathbb E[S]\), which pins the natural parameter, and to note that the \(x\)-dependence of the mixture density is absorbed into \(c(x,\phi)\) while the \(x\theta/\phi\) term reproduces the \(e^{-cx}\cdot x^{\,\cdot}\) structure.

With \(b(\theta)=\tfrac{\gamma+1}{\gamma}\big(\tfrac{-\theta}{\gamma+1}\big)^{-\gamma}\),

and since \(\tfrac{-\theta}{\gamma+1}=\big(\tfrac{\lambda\gamma}{c}\big)^{-1/(\gamma+1)}\),

which is exactly \(\mathbb E[S]=\mathbb E[N]\,\mathbb E[Y_1]=\lambda\cdot\tfrac{\gamma}{c}\). The natural parameter \(\theta\) and dispersion \(\phi\) given in the problem are therefore the correct EDF coordinates; matching the leading \(\exp\{(x\theta-b(\theta))/\phi\}\) factor against \(e^{-cx}\) fixes \(\theta\propto -c^{1/(\gamma+1)}\) as stated, and the residual normalising series is precisely \(c(x,\phi)\).

1.3Tweedie's role in reserving›

Aggregate claims per cell (accident year × development year, or per risk class) are naturally zero-inflated and continuous-when-positive: many cells have no claim, positive cells are right-skewed amounts. The Tweedie family — compound Poisson–gamma, power parameter \(p\in(1,2)\) — has exactly this structure: a mass at \(0\) and a gamma-like continuous part, all inside one exponential dispersion family. That makes it a legitimate GLM response, so a single likelihood jointly captures claim frequency and severity and lets you regress reserves/premiums on covariates by maximum likelihood, with all the EDF machinery (deviance, IRLS, standard errors) coming for free. It is the standard bridge between the frequency–severity split and one-model pricing/reserving.

The technique playbook

Almost every subpart on this exam is one of nine moves. Each card below is a trigger — the phrasing you'll actually see — followed by the steps and the exact results to name. Reading the trigger should already start your pen moving.

R1"Argue why \(\hat\theta_n\) is a natural / consistent estimator" — a plug-in average.
  1. Write it as a sample mean of iid bounded functions of the data: \(\hat\theta_n=\tfrac1n\sum_i g(Z_i)\).
  2. Invoke the LLN: \(\hat\theta_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mathbb E[g(Z_1)]=\theta\). Say what it targets in one line.
  3. If \(\hat\theta_n=H(\text{averages})\) for continuous \(H\) (a ratio, a log), add the continuous mapping theorem.
Cite: LLN · CMT (1.2) · for a whole CDF use Glivenko–Cantelli (1.7)
R2"Show \(\int \varphi\,dF^{(n)}\xrightarrow{P}\int\varphi\,dF\)" — consistency of an integral against an empirical / KM measure.

This is the archetype behind Problem 2.4. Split \(\int_0^\infty=\int_0^M+\int_M^\infty\) and handle the two halves with two different tools:

  1. Bulk (compact \([0,M]\)): uniform consistency \(\sup_t|F^{(n)}-F|\xrightarrow{P}0\) (extended Glivenko–Cantelli / KM strong consistency) \(\Rightarrow\) weak convergence of measures \(\Rightarrow\int_0^M\varphi\,dF^{(n)}\xrightarrow{P}\int_0^M\varphi\,dF\).
  2. Tail: a uniform-integrability bound — Mauro's inequality \(\mathbb E[\int\varphi\mathbf 1_{>M}\,dF^{(n)}]\le\int\varphi\mathbf 1_{>M}\,dF\to0\) as \(M\to\infty\), uniformly in \(n\).
  3. Send \(n\to\infty\) then \(M\to\infty\).
Cite: extended GC (1.20) / KM consistency · Mauro's inequality
R3"Find the limit law at a point" — a single number \(\sqrt n(\hat\theta_n-\theta)\).
  1. Express \(\hat\theta_n=h(\bar A_n)\) as a smooth function of one or more averages.
  2. Apply the CLT to the averages: \(\sqrt n(\bar A_n-a)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\Sigma)\) (Laplace / Lindeberg–Feller).
  3. Push through the delta method: \(\sqrt n(h(\bar A_n)-h(a))\xrightarrow{d}\nabla h(a)^\top N(0,\Sigma)\).
Cite: Laplace CLT (1.4) · delta method (1.10) · Slutsky (1.3) to drop lower-order terms
R4"Show weak convergence of the whole process / identify the limit variance."
  1. Finite-dimensional distributions: fix a grid \(t_1,\dots,t_M\); reduce to a 1-D CLT with the Cramér–Wold device.
  2. Tightness: show the indexing class is \(P\)-Donsker via a finite bracketing entropy integral \(\int_0^1\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}}\,d\varepsilon\lt\infty\).
  3. Together \(\Rightarrow\) a tight centred Gaussian limit. Read off the covariance from step 1.
  4. If the target is a transform of \(F^{(n)}\) (hazard, quantile), route the bridge through the functional delta method.
Cite: Cramér–Wold · bracketing CLT / Donsker (1.28–1.29) · bridge (1.30) · functional delta (1.26)
R5"Compute the covariance / variance of the limit."

For indicator-based statistics the one identity you need is

so \(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf 1_{Z\le s},\mathbf 1_{Z\le t})=F(s\wedge t)-F(s)F(t)\). For a Markov / jump process, condition: \(\mathbb P(Z_s=j,Z_t=j)=p_j(s)P_{jj}(s,t)\) for \(s\le t\). Sanity check: the diagonal must collapse to the known pointwise variance, e.g. \(p_j(t)(1-p_j(t))\).

Cite: covariance-of-indicators identity · Markov property
R6"The data are right-censored" — the plain estimator is biased; adapt it.
  1. State the bias. \(\min(Z,C)\le Z\) chops the large values \(\Rightarrow\) downward bias; you're estimating \(\mathbb E[W]\ne\mathbb E[Z]\).
  2. Single lifetime: inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting \(\delta_\ell/(1-G^{(n)}(W_\ell-))\), which reconstructs Kaplan–Meier (Satten–Datta: \(K^{(n)}\equiv F^{(n)}\)).
  3. Multistate: estimate intensities from what you see (Nelson–Aalen), then the transition matrix by the product integral \(\hat P(0,t)=\prod_{(0,t]}(\mathrm{Id}+d\hat A)\) (Aalen–Johansen).
Cite: Kaplan–Meier · IPCW / Satten–Datta · Aalen–Johansen · telescoping product identity
R7"Classify the tail" — heavy-tailed / subexponential / regularly varying.
  1. Regular variation: check \(\bar F(tx)/\bar F(x)\to t^{-1/\gamma}\); the exponent is the tail index \(1/\gamma\) (Burr \(\to\beta\eta\)).
  2. Remember the strict, one-way chain: RV \(\Rightarrow\) subexponential \(\Rightarrow\) long-tailed \(\Rightarrow\) heavy-tailed.
  3. For a product \(ZW\), use Breiman's lemma: same index, constant \(\mathbb E[W^{1/\gamma}]\).
  4. Uniform tail control comes from Potter's bounds.
Cite: RV\(\Rightarrow\)subexp\(\Rightarrow\)long\(\Rightarrow\)heavy (wk5 Ex1) · Potter (3.5–3.6) · Breiman (3.32)
R8"Choose / justify a bandwidth" — kernel density or regression.
  1. Write AMSE = (squared bias \(\propto h^4\)) + (variance \(\propto 1/(nh)\)).
  2. Differentiate in \(h\), set to zero \(\Rightarrow\ \hat h\propto n^{-1/5}\); check the second-order condition.
  3. Back-substitute: the optimal AMSE decays at \(n^{-4/5}\) — strictly slower than the parametric \(n^{-1}\). That gap is the price of assuming nothing about shape.
  4. In practice pick \(h\) by minimising the cross-validation risk \(\hat J(h)\).
Cite: Priestley–Chao AMSE (Thm 2.3, wk4 Ex2) · CV risk
R9"Show membership in the exponential dispersion family."
  1. Target form \(f(x;\theta,\phi)=\exp\{(x\theta-b(\theta))/\phi+c(x,\phi)\}\).
  2. Match the \(x\theta/\phi\) term against the density's \(e^{-cx}x^{\,\cdot}\) structure to fix \(\theta\); dump the awkward normalising series into \(c(x,\phi)\).
  3. Verify the mean identity \(b'(\theta)=\mathbb E[S]\): this single check certifies the parametrisation without re-deriving it.
Cite: EDF definition · mean identity \(b'(\theta)=\mu\) (Problem 1.2)

Standard tricks worth memorising

Exam-day tips

Three hours, three equal-weight problems, closed book, exact references required. The score is won on discipline as much as on cleverness.

1

Harvest the independent subparts first. Subparts are engineered so later ones use earlier results, not your ability to prove them. If 2.2 is hard, still use "\(K^{(n)}=F^{(n)}\)" to answer 2.3 and 2.4. Bank every "argue why" and every consistency mark before wrestling a hard covariance.

2

Name the theorem — that's most of the mark. "By the bracketing CLT (1.28)" earns more than a page of hand-waving. Keep the citation list from §24 on the tip of your pen; a proof is largely a sequence of correct names.

3

Keep "argue why" answers to 2–3 sentences. These want the mechanism, not a proof: what the estimator targets by the LLN, or precisely where a bias enters. Don't over-write and don't leave them blank.

4

Recognise the integral-consistency archetype (R2). Any \(\int\varphi\,dF^{(n)}\to\int\varphi\,dF\) is uniform consistency on compacts + a uniform-integrability tail bound. Write those two words and you've structured the whole proof.

5

Sanity-check every covariance on the diagonal. A limit variance process must reduce to the known pointwise variance when \(s=t\) — e.g. \(F(t)(1-F(t))\) or \(p_j(t)(1-p_j(t))\). If it doesn't, you have a sign or a min/max error.

6

Mind the signs and the given constants. In the EDF problem \(\theta\lt0\) is stated — respect it. Don't re-derive a reparametrisation under time pressure; verify \(b'(\theta)=\mu\) and move on.

7

Censoring is a flag, not a wall. The instant you see \(\min(Z,C)\), reach for KM / IPCW (single lifetime) or Aalen–Johansen (multistate). State the downward bias of the naïve estimator in one line first.

8

Rehearse the four reflexes cold. Consistency? LLN/GC + CMT. Point limit? \(h(\text{average})\) + delta. Path limit? fin-dim CLT + Donsker + functional delta. Covariance? indicator identity. Everything on the paper is a recombination of these.

V1 · R9Show the Gamma law is in the EDF and find its variance function›

Problem. Write \(Y\sim\Gamma\) with shape \(\nu\) and mean \(\mu\) (so rate \(=\nu/\mu\)). Show \(Y\) belongs to the exponential dispersion family, identify \((\theta,\phi,b)\), and compute the variance function \(V(\mu)\).

Method (R9). Take logs, isolate the term linear in \(x\) to read \(\theta/\phi\), collect the rest.

The density is \(f(x)=\dfrac{(\nu/\mu)^\nu x^{\nu-1}e^{-\nu x/\mu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\), so

\[\log f(x)=\underbrace{-\tfrac{x}{\mu}-\log\mu}_{\text{depends on }\mu}\cdot\;\nu+(\nu-1)\log x+\nu\log\nu-\log\Gamma(\nu).\]

The \(x\)-linear part is \(\nu(-x/\mu)=\dfrac{x\,(-1/\mu)}{1/\nu}\), so read off \(\theta=-1/\mu\;(\lt0)\) and \(\phi=1/\nu\). The remaining \(\mu\)-dependent constant \(-\nu\log\mu\) equals \(-b(\theta)/\phi\), giving \(b(\theta)=\log\mu=-\log(-\theta)\); everything in \(x\) alone is \(c(x,\phi)\).

Certify and differentiate. \(b'(\theta)=-1/\theta=\mu=\mathbb E[Y]\) ✓, and \(b''(\theta)=1/\theta^2=\mu^2\). Hence

\[V(\mu)=b''(\theta)=\mu^2,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y)=\phi\,V(\mu)=\mu^2/\nu.\]

Transfers to: Problem 1.2, and any "show \(\in\) EDF / find the variance function." Poisson gives \(\theta=\log\mu,\ b=e^\theta,\ V(\mu)=\mu\); inverse Gaussian gives \(V(\mu)=\mu^3\); the Tweedie ladder is \(V(\mu)=\mu^p\).

V2 · R4Asymptotic variance of the sample quantile / Value-at-Risk›

Problem. Let \(q_p=F^{-1}(p)\) with \(F\) differentiable, \(f(q_p)\gt0\). Find the limit law of \(\sqrt n\big(q^{(n)}_p-q_p\big)\), where \(q^{(n)}_p=[F^{(n)}]^{-1}(p)\).

Method (R4). Route the empirical bridge through the inversion map with the functional delta method.

Start from the bridge \(\sqrt n(F^{(n)}-F)\xrightarrow{d}B\circ F\). The Hadamard derivative of the inverse map \(\Psi(F)=F^{-1}\) at \(p\) is \(h\mapsto -\,h(q_p)/f(q_p)\). Feeding \(B\circ F\) through,

\[\sqrt n\big(q^{(n)}_p-q_p\big)\xrightarrow{d}-\frac{(B\circ F)(q_p)}{f(q_p)}=-\frac{B(p)}{f(q_p)},\]

since \(F(q_p)=p\). As \(\operatorname{Var}(B(p))=p(1-p)\),

\[\sqrt n\big(q^{(n)}_p-q_p\big)\xrightarrow{d}N\!\Big(0,\ \tfrac{p(1-p)}{f(q_p)^2}\Big).\]

Transfers to: any smooth functional of \(F^{(n)}\). \(\mathrm{VaR}_p\) is a quantile, so the same formula prices its uncertainty; the cumulative hazard route gives \(F(t)/(1-F(t))\).

V3 · R5Identify the joint limit covariance of the empirical CDF›

Problem. For \(s\le t\), give the limit covariance of \(\sqrt n(F^{(n)}(s)-F(s))\) and \(\sqrt n(F^{(n)}(t)-F(t))\).

Method (R5). The one identity: \(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf 1_A,\mathbf 1_B)=\mathbb P(A\cap B)-\mathbb P(A)\mathbb P(B)\).

With \(A=\{Z\le s\}\), \(B=\{Z\le t\}\) and \(A\cap B=\{Z\le s\}\) (as \(s\le t\)),

\[\operatorname{Cov}=F(s)-F(s)F(t)=F(s\wedge t)-F(s)F(t).\]

The diagonal \(s=t\) collapses to \(F(t)(1-F(t))\) — the sanity check every covariance must pass.

Transfers to: every "identify \(\Sigma\)" subpart, and the fidi half of the bridge theorem (Week 1, Ex 2; Week 2, Ex 9).

V4 · R5Limit variance process for a two-state occupation›

Problem. An alive/dead Markov process; \(p_1(t)=\mathbb P(Z_t=1)\) estimated by \(p^{(n)}_1(t)\). Identify the covariance of the limit process \(\gamma\) of \(\sqrt n(p^{(n)}_1-p_1)\).

Method (R5 + Markov). Indicator identity, then condition on the earlier time.

For \(s\le t\), using the Markov property \(\mathbb P(Z_s=1,Z_t=1)=p_1(s)P_{11}(s,t)\),

\[\operatorname{Cov}(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))=p_1(s)P_{11}(s,t)-p_1(s)p_1(t).\]

On the diagonal this is \(p_1(t)(1-p_1(t))\), a Bernoulli variance — exactly the sanity check.

Transfers to: Problem 3.4 verbatim, and any occupation or transition-count CLT where you must "identify the limit variance process."

V5 · R2 + R6Consistency of a Kaplan–Meier mean under censoring›

Problem. With right-censoring \(W=\min(Z,C)\), show the KM-based estimator \(\hat\mu^{(n)}=\int_0^\infty w\,dF^{(n)}(w)\) satisfies \(\hat\mu^{(n)}\xrightarrow{P}\mathbb E[Z]\) when \(\mathbb E[Z]\lt\infty\).

Method. First de-bias (R6), then the integral-consistency archetype (R2).

1 — why not the naïve mean. \(\tfrac1n\sum W_\ell\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mathbb E[\min(Z,C)]\le\mathbb E[Z]\): censoring chops the large \(Z\)'s, a downward bias. IPCW reweighting reconstructs KM (Satten–Datta), so \(\hat\mu^{(n)}=\int w\,dF^{(n)}\).

2 — split and bound. Write \(\int_0^\infty=\int_0^M+\int_M^\infty\). On \([0,M]\), uniform KM consistency \(\sup_t|F^{(n)}-F|\xrightarrow{P}0\) plus extended continuous mapping give \(\int_0^M w\,dF^{(n)}\xrightarrow{P}\int_0^M w\,dF\). For the tail, Markov then Mauro:

\[\mathbb P\Big(\!\int_M^\infty\! w\,dF^{(n)}\gt\eta\Big)\le\tfrac1\eta\,\mathbb E\Big[\!\int_M^\infty\! w\,dF^{(n)}\Big]\le\tfrac1\eta\!\int_M^\infty\! w\,dF\xrightarrow{M\to\infty}0,\]

uniformly in \(n\). Let \(n\to\infty\) then \(M\to\infty\): \(\hat\mu^{(n)}\xrightarrow{P}\int_0^\infty w\,dF=\mathbb E[Z]\).

Transfers to: all of Problem 2, and any censored functional \(\int\varphi\,dF^{(n)}\). Memorise the two-word skeleton: uniform-on-compacts + UI tail.

V6 · R7Classify a Weibull tail and find its mean excess›

Problem. \(\bar F(z)=e^{-(z/\lambda)^\tau}\), \(\tau,\lambda\gt0\). For which \(\tau\) is \(Z\) heavy-tailed, and what is the mean-excess growth?

Method (R7). Heavy-tailed \(\Leftrightarrow\) the MGF is infinite for every \(t\gt0\), i.e. \(e^{\varepsilon z}\bar F(z)\to\infty\).

\(e^{\varepsilon z}\bar F(z)=\exp\{\varepsilon z-(z/\lambda)^\tau\}\). If \(\tau\lt1\) the linear term dominates \(\Rightarrow\to\infty\Rightarrow\) heavy-tailed (in fact subexponential). If \(\tau\ge1\) the MGF is finite \(\Rightarrow\) light-tailed.

The mean excess, by Watson's lemma on \(\int_u^\infty\bar F\), is

\[e_F(u)=\frac{\int_u^\infty\bar F}{\bar F(u)}\sim\frac{\lambda^\tau}{\tau}\,u^{1-\tau}\quad(u\to\infty):\]

increasing (heavy) for \(\tau\lt1\), the constant \(\lambda\) (memoryless) at \(\tau=1\), decreasing (light) for \(\tau\gt1\). The slope of the mean-excess plot is the diagnostic.

Transfers to: the Pareto/lognormal computations of Week 1 and any "classify the tail" — apply the same MGF test and read the mean-excess signature.

V7 · R7Tail of a heavy risk scaled by an independent light factor›

Problem. \(Z\) is Pareto with tail index \(\alpha\) (so \(\bar F(z)=(\kappa/z)^\alpha\)); \(W\ge0\) is independent with \(\mathbb E[W^{\alpha+\varepsilon}]\lt\infty\) for some \(\varepsilon\gt0\). Find the tail of the product \(ZW\) (e.g. a claim scaled by a random inflation factor).

Method (R7). Breiman's lemma — regular variation is preserved under multiplication by a lighter factor.

\[\lim_{z\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb P(ZW\gt z)}{\mathbb P(Z\gt z)}=\mathbb E[W^{\alpha}],\]

so \(ZW\) is regularly varying with the same index \(\alpha\); only the constant is rescaled by \(\mathbb E[W^\alpha]\). The proof dominates the ratio via Potter's bound and applies dominated convergence.

Transfers to: Week 5's Breiman exercise; exposure- or inflation-scaled losses, and any product of a heavy and a light risk.

V8 · R8Optimal bandwidth and rate for a kernel density estimator›

Problem. For \(f^{(n)}(x)=\tfrac1{nh}\sum_iK\!\big(\tfrac{x-X_i}{h}\big)\), give the leading bias and variance, the AMISE-optimal bandwidth, and the resulting rate.

Method (R8). Bias\(^2\propto h^4\), variance \(\propto1/(nh)\); minimise their sum.

\[\mathrm{bias}\approx\tfrac{h^2}{2}f''(x)\!\int u^2K,\qquad \operatorname{Var}\approx\frac{f(x)}{nh}\!\int K^2.\]

Minimising the integrated version \(\mathrm{AMISE}=\tfrac{h^4}{4}(\!\int u^2K)^2\!\int(f'')^2+\tfrac{\int K^2}{nh}\) gives

\[\hat h=\Big(\tfrac{\int K^2}{(\int u^2K)^2\int(f'')^2}\Big)^{1/5}n^{-1/5},\qquad \mathrm{AMISE}(\hat h)\asymp n^{-4/5}.\]

The \(n^{-4/5}\) rate is slower than the parametric \(n^{-1}\) — the price of assuming nothing about \(f\).

Transfers to: Week 4 (Priestley–Chao); the Nadaraya–Watson smoother, with \(f(x)\) replaced by \(\sigma^2(x)/f(x)\) in the variance.

V9 · R3CLT for the sample variance via the delta method›

Problem. Show \(\sqrt n(\hat\sigma^2_n-\sigma^2)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\mu_{(4)}-\mu_{(2)}^2)\), where \(\mu_{(k)}\) are central moments and \(\mu_{(2)}=\sigma^2\).

Method (R3). Write the estimator as a smooth function of averages, then apply the delta method.

With sample raw moments \(\hat m_1=\bar X\), \(\hat m_2=\tfrac1n\sum X_i^2\), we have \(\hat\sigma^2_n=\hat m_2-\hat m_1^2=:h(\hat m_1,\hat m_2)\). The bivariate CLT gives \(\sqrt n(\hat m_1-m_1,\hat m_2-m_2)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\Sigma)\), and \(\nabla h=(-2m_1,1)\). The delta method yields a centred Gaussian with variance \(\nabla h^\top\Sigma\,\nabla h=\mu_{(4)}-\mu_{(2)}^2\).

Transfers to: any moment-based or plug-in estimator (skewness, correlation, the mean-excess estimator) — the pointwise "write as \(h(\text{average})\), then delta" archetype.

V10 · R6Nelson–Aalen estimator and its variance under censoring›

Problem. From right-censored data \((W_\ell,\delta_\ell)\), the Nelson–Aalen estimator of the cumulative hazard is \(\hat\Lambda(t)=\int_0^t \tfrac{dN(s)}{Y(s)}\), where \(N(s)=\sum_\ell\mathbf 1_{W_\ell\le s,\delta_\ell=1}\) counts events and \(Y(s)=\sum_\ell\mathbf 1_{W_\ell\ge s}\) is the number at risk. Describe its jumps, argue consistency, and give a variance estimator.

Method (R6 + counting processes). Increments come only from the at-risk / event bookkeeping.

Jumps. \(dN(s)=0\) except at observed event times, so \(\hat\Lambda\) is a step function jumping by \(d_i/Y_i\) at the \(i\)-th event time (\(d_i\) tied events, \(Y_i\) at risk). Censored times only decrement \(Y\); they never add a jump.

Consistency. \(N/n\) and \(Y/n\) converge uniformly to their means (Glivenko–Cantelli), and \(\hat\Lambda(t)\xrightarrow{P}\Lambda(t)=\int_0^t\tfrac{dF}{1-F_-}=-\log(1-F(t))\) for continuous \(F\), on any \(t\) with \((1-F(t))(1-G(t))\gt0\).

Variance (Aalen). The natural plug-in is \(\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}\,\hat\Lambda(t)=\sum_{t_i\le t}\tfrac{d_i}{Y_i^2}\). With no censoring this limits to \(\int_0^t\tfrac{dF}{(1-F)^2}=\tfrac{F(t)}{1-F(t)}\) — the same pointwise hazard variance the functional delta gives.

Transfers to: Problem 2's survival machinery; any "describe the estimator's jumps / state its variance" for KM or Nelson–Aalen.

V11 · R8AMSE-optimal bandwidth for the Nadaraya–Watson / conditional-CDF smoother›

Problem. For the Nadaraya–Watson estimator \(m^{(n)}(x)\) of \(m(x)=\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x]\), give the leading bias and variance and the optimal bandwidth. Then state the analogous limit for the conditional CDF \(F^{(n)}(t\mid x)\).

Method (R8). Same bias\(^2\)–variance trade as the density case, with the conditional variance in the numerator.

\[\mathrm{bias}\approx\tfrac{h^2}{2}\Big(m''(x)+\tfrac{2m'(x)f'(x)}{f(x)}\Big)\!\int u^2K,\qquad \operatorname{Var}\approx\frac{\sigma^2(x)}{f(x)\,nh}\!\int K^2,\]

where \(\sigma^2(x)=\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=x)\). Minimising the AMSE gives \(\hat h\propto n^{-1/5}\) and \(\mathrm{AMSE}\asymp n^{-4/5}\), the familiar nonparametric rate. For the conditional CDF, replace \(Y\) by \(\mathbf 1_{Y\le t}\): the "variance" becomes \(F(t\mid x)(1-F(t\mid x))\) and

\[\sqrt{nh}\,\big(F^{(n)}(\cdot\mid x)-F(\cdot\mid x)\big)\xrightarrow{d}\Big(\tfrac{\int K^2}{f(x)}\Big)^{1/2}B\circ F(\cdot\mid x).\]

Transfers to: the distributional-regression section (§17) and Week 4; any kernel smoother — only the numerator of the variance changes.

V12 · R7Regular variation is closed under maxima and sums — the single big jump›

Problem. Let \(Z_1,\dots,Z_n\) be iid, regularly varying with index \(1/\gamma\), \(M_n=\max_i Z_i\), \(S_n=\sum_i Z_i\). Show \(M_n\) and \(S_n\) have the same tail order as a single \(Z\), scaled by \(n\).

Method (R7). Maxima are elementary; sums use subexponentiality.

Maximum. \(\mathbb P(M_n\gt z)=1-(1-\bar F(z))^n\sim n\,\bar F(z)\) as \(z\to\infty\), so \(M_n\) is regularly varying with the same index \(1/\gamma\).

Sum. Regular variation \(\Rightarrow\) subexponential (Week 5, Ex 1), whose defining property is exactly

\[\mathbb P(S_n\gt z)\sim n\,\bar F(z)\sim \mathbb P(M_n\gt z)\quad(z\to\infty).\]

So the sum is large essentially because one term is large — the single-big-jump principle. For insurance this is the whole story: aggregate losses in a heavy-tailed line are driven by the one catastrophic claim, not the accumulation of many.

Transfers to: Week 5's subexponential chain; any "closure of regular variation" question and the intuition behind tail reserves.

Intuition builders

Four mental models that make the proofs feel inevitable rather than arbitrary. If you can narrate these in your own words, the machinery stops being a list of theorems and becomes one picture.

Why heavy tails break the average

The whole reason insurance needs a separate theory of tails is that the sample mean — the workhorse of the CLT — stops behaving when claims are heavy. Push the tail index below: with a light (exponential) tail the running mean settles quickly; with a heavy (Pareto) tail it lurches every time a rare giant claim lands, and never quite settles. Both lines have the same true mean of 1.

At \(\alpha\le2\) the Pareto variance is infinite, so the CLT gives no usable error bar; at \(\alpha\le1\) even the mean is infinite. This is precisely why Week 5 replaces "average \(\pm\) CLT" with regular variation, mean-excess and the Hill estimator.

Why \(\sqrt n\), and why a bridge

Center and it wobbles; scale by \(\sqrt n\) and the wobble stabilises into a Gaussian of fixed width — too little scaling and it collapses to 0, too much and it blows up. Now do it at every \(t\) at once. The curve \(F^{(n)}-F\) is pinned to 0 at both ends (nothing below the smallest value, everything below the largest), and its variance \(F(t)(1-F(t))\) bulges in the middle. A Gaussian process pinned at both ends with that variance is exactly a Brownian bridge \(B\circ F\). Every path limit in the course is this one picture, transformed.

Censoring is "missing at the top"

Censoring removes the largest observations — the ones still running when observation stops — so a naïve average is biased downward. Kaplan–Meier and IPCW don't invent the missing data; they reweight the survivors to stand in for those who were cut off, which is why the reweighted measure exactly reconstructs the estimator you'd have had with full data (Satten–Datta). Same idea, one dimension up: Aalen–Johansen reweights transition counts by who is still at risk.

Bias and variance are one dial

Every smoother has a single knob \(h\). Turn it up and you average over a wide window: low variance, high bias (you blur real features). Turn it down and you track the data closely: low bias, high variance (you chase noise). The optimal \(h\propto n^{-1/5}\) is just where the two curves cross, and the leftover \(n^{-4/5}\) rate — slower than the parametric \(n^{-1}\) — is the standing tax for refusing to assume a shape.

Two fresh problems in the exact style and breadth of the real paper — an empirical-process problem and a heavy-tails problem — each with independently-attackable subparts. Sit them closed-book with a three-hour clock in mind (about 50 minutes each), then reveal.

Mock Problem A — Value-at-Risk and Expected Shortfall as empirical functionals

Losses \(Z_1,\dots,Z_n\) iid with continuous cdf \(F\), density \(f\gt0\). For \(p\in(0,1)\) write \(\mathrm{VaR}_p=q_p=F^{-1}(p)\) and \(\mathrm{ES}_p=\mathbb E[Z\mid Z\gt q_p]\). Estimators use the empirical cdf \(F^{(n)}\).

A.1Consistency of empirical VaR›

\(q^{(n)}_p=[F^{(n)}]^{-1}(p)\). By Glivenko–Cantelli \(\sup_t|F^{(n)}(t)-F(t)|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0\), and since \(F\) is continuous and strictly increasing near \(q_p\) (as \(f(q_p)\gt0\)), the inverse map is continuous there. The continuous mapping theorem gives \(q^{(n)}_p\xrightarrow{P}q_p\). This is recipe R1 with the inversion continuity added.

A.2CLT for empirical VaR›

Route the bridge through the inverse map (functional delta, R4). With \(\sqrt n(F^{(n)}-F)\xrightarrow{d}B\circ F\) and the Hadamard derivative of inversion \(h\mapsto -h(q_p)/f(q_p)\),

\[\sqrt n\big(q^{(n)}_p-q_p\big)\xrightarrow{d}-\frac{(B\circ F)(q_p)}{f(q_p)}=-\frac{B(p)}{f(q_p)}\sim N\!\Big(0,\tfrac{p(1-p)}{f(q_p)^2}\Big),\]

using \(F(q_p)=p\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(B(p))=p(1-p)\).

A.3Expected Shortfall as an integral against \(F^{(n)}\)›

Since \(\mathrm{ES}_p=\tfrac1{1-p}\int_{q_p}^\infty z\,dF(z)\), the plug-in is

\[\mathrm{ES}^{(n)}_p=\frac1{1-p}\int_{q^{(n)}_p}^\infty z\,dF^{(n)}(z)=\frac1{n(1-p)}\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i\,\mathbf 1_{Z_i\gt q^{(n)}_p}.\]

Consistency. \(q^{(n)}_p\xrightarrow{P}q_p\) (A.1) and \(F^{(n)}\Rightarrow F\) uniformly, so by the integral-consistency archetype (R2) — weak convergence on the compact bulk plus a uniform-integrability tail bound using \(\mathbb E[Z]\lt\infty\) — \(\mathrm{ES}^{(n)}_p\xrightarrow{P}\mathrm{ES}_p\).

A.4Why ES is coherent and VaR is not›

Expected shortfall averages the loss beyond the quantile, so it is subadditive: \(\mathrm{ES}_p(X+Y)\le\mathrm{ES}_p(X)+\mathrm{ES}_p(Y)\), rewarding diversification, and it satisfies the other coherence axioms (monotonicity, homogeneity, translation-invariance). VaR is only a quantile and can increase under pooling — merging two portfolios can push a fat-tailed loss just past the threshold in a way each alone did not — so it violates subadditivity. That single failure is why Solvency II and Basel shifted capital measures toward ES.

Mock Problem B — Regular variation, mean excess and the Hill estimator

Claims \(Z_1,\dots,Z_n\) iid with \(\bar F(z)=z^{-1/\gamma}\ell(z)\) regularly varying, \(\gamma\in(0,1)\), \(\ell\) slowly varying. Order statistics \(Z_{(1)}\le\cdots\le Z_{(n)}\).

B.1Mean excess grows linearly; read off \(\gamma\)›

For a regularly varying (Pareto-type) tail the mean excess satisfies \(e_F(u)/u\to\gamma/(1-\gamma)\) as \(u\to\infty\). Concretely for exact Pareto \(\bar F(z)=(\kappa/z)^{1/\gamma}\) with index \(\alpha=1/\gamma\gt1\), the Week 1 computation gives \(e_F(u)=u/(\alpha-1)=\tfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}u\), exactly linear. Diagnostic: a straight, upward-sloping mean-excess plot signals a heavy (regularly varying) tail, and the slope pins \(\gamma\).

B.2The Hill estimator and its integral form›

The Hill estimator of \(\gamma\) on the top \(k\) order statistics is

\[\hat\gamma_{n,k}=\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log\frac{Z_{(n-i+1)}}{Z_{(n-k)}}.\]

Writing \(\hat T_n(s)=\tfrac1k\sum_i\mathbf 1_{Z_i\gt Z_{(n-k)}s}\), the same estimator is the integral \(\int_1^\infty \tfrac{\hat T_n(s)}{s}\,ds\); telescoping the indicators over the top \(k\) order statistics recovers the sum form (Week 5, Ex 2). It is the empirical analogue of \(\gamma=\int_1^\infty \tfrac{T(s)}{s}\,ds\) with \(T(s)=s^{-1/\gamma}\).

B.3Consistency and asymptotic normality›

As \(n\to\infty\) with \(k=k_n\to\infty\) and \(k/n\to0\) (an intermediate sequence), \(\hat\gamma_{n,k}\xrightarrow{P}\gamma\): the top \(k\) log-spacings are approximately iid exponential with mean \(\gamma\), so their average concentrates. Under a second-order regular-variation condition,

\[\sqrt k\,\big(\hat\gamma_{n,k}-\gamma\big)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\gamma^2).\]

Bias–variance in \(k\): small \(k\) means high variance (few tail points); large \(k\) reaches into the body and biases the slowly-varying part. The optimal \(k\) balances the two — the heavy-tail echo of bandwidth selection.

B.4Tail of an inflated claim›

Let \(W\ge0\) be an independent inflation factor with \(\mathbb E[W^{1/\gamma+\varepsilon}]\lt\infty\). By Breiman's lemma the inflated claim \(ZW\) is regularly varying with the same index \(1/\gamma\), and

\[\mathbb P(ZW\gt z)\sim \mathbb E[W^{1/\gamma}]\,\mathbb P(Z\gt z).\]

Inflation rescales the tail constant by \(\mathbb E[W^{1/\gamma}]\) but leaves the index — and hence the Hill estimate and the reserve's tail shape — unchanged.
