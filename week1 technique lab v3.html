PortmanteauConvergenceThm 1.1

Mechanism. Indicators are not continuous, so you cannot test them directly. Approximate \(\mathbf 1_G\) from below by explicit Lipschitz functions built from the distance to \(G^c\) — open sets are exactly those where this one-sided approximation works.

you have set probabilitiesproving CMT or an approximation theorem
Statement
\(Z_n\xrightarrow{d}Z\iff\liminf_nP(Z_n\in G)\ge P(Z\in G)\ \forall G\) open \(\iff\limsup_nP(Z_n\in F)\le P(Z\in F)\ \forall F\) closed.
Full derivation — every line
  1. Build the approximating functions. For \(G\) open put \[f_m(s)=\min\{1,\;m\,d(s,G^c)\},\qquad m\ge1.\] Why legal: \(s\mapsto d(s,G^c)\) is 1-Lipschitz, so \(f_m\) is \(m\)-Lipschitz, hence continuous and bounded. Also \(0\le f_m\le\mathbf 1_G\) (if \(s\notin G\) then \(d=0\)), and \(f_m\uparrow\mathbf 1_G\) pointwise because \(G\) open \(\Rightarrow d(s,G^c)>0\) for \(s\in G\).
  2. One-sided bound at level \(n\). Since \(f_m\le\mathbf 1_G\), \[E[f_m(Z_n)]\le P(Z_n\in G).\]
  3. Take \(\liminf_n\). The left side converges by assumption: \[E[f_m(Z)]=\lim_n E[f_m(Z_n)]\le\liminf_n P(Z_n\in G).\] Why: \(f_m\in C_b\), so \(\xrightarrow{d}\) applies. The right-hand side does not depend on \(m\).
  4. Take \(m\to\infty\). Monotone convergence gives \(E[f_m(Z)]\uparrow P(Z\in G)\), hence \[P(Z\in G)\le\liminf_n P(Z_n\in G).\]
  5. Closed sets by complement. With \(F=G^c\), \(P(Z_n\in F)=1-P(Z_n\in G)\) and \(\liminf(-a_n)=-\limsup a_n\) flips the inequality.
  6. Converse, step 1 (non-negative \(f\)). For continuous \(f\ge0\), the set \(\{f>t\}\) is open for each \(t>0\), so the assumption gives \(P(f(Z)>t)\le\liminf_nP(f(Z_n)>t)\). Then \[E[f(Z)]=\int_0^\infty P(f(Z)>t)\,dt\le\int_0^\infty\liminf_n P(f(Z_n)>t)\,dt\le\liminf_n\int_0^\infty P(f(Z_n)>t)\,dt=\liminf_nE[f(Z_n)].\] Why: first equality is the layer-cake identity; the last inequality is Fatou applied to the functions \(t\mapsto P(f(Z_n)>t)\).
  7. Converse, step 2 (bounded \(f\)). Let \(|f|\le c\). Apply step 6 to the non-negative functions \(c+f\) and \(c-f\): \[c+E[f(Z)]\le\liminf_n\big(c+E[f(Z_n)]\big),\qquad c-E[f(Z)]\le\liminf_n\big(c-E[f(Z_n)]\big).\] The first gives \(E[f(Z)]\le\liminf_nE[f(Z_n)]\); the second gives \(\limsup_nE[f(Z_n)]\le E[f(Z)]\). Together \(E[f(Z_n)]\to E[f(Z)]\). The trick: "apply the one-sided result to \(c\pm f\)" converts a one-sided inequality into a two-sided limit. It reappears in every proof where you only have a \(\liminf\) bound.
Transfers to
CMT (pull a closed set back through \(g\)) · Theorem 1.5 (uses closed \(\varepsilon\)-enlargements \(F^\varepsilon\)) · \(Z_n\xrightarrow{d}c\Rightarrow Z_n\xrightarrow{P}c\) · tightness/Prokhorov arguments in metric spaces.
Pitfall
Equality for a general Borel \(A\) needs \(P(Z\in\partial A)=0\). The asymmetry (liminf for open, limsup for closed) is not cosmetic — it is what the one-sided approximation buys you.

The subsequence trickConvergenceLem. 1.2 proof

Mechanism. A real sequence converges to \(L\) iff every subsequence has a further subsequence converging to \(L\). This lets you upgrade \(\xrightarrow{P}\) to a.s. along a sub-subsequence, where pointwise tools (dominated convergence) become available.

you need a.s. tools but have only \(\xrightarrow{P}\)
Full derivation — \(\xrightarrow{P}\Rightarrow\xrightarrow{d}\)
  1. Fix a test function and an arbitrary subsequence. Let \(f\) be bounded continuous and take any subsequence \((Z_{n_k})\). Goal: show \(E[f(Z_{n_k})]\) has a further subsequence converging to \(E f(Z)\).
  2. Extract a fast sub-subsequence. Since \(Z_{n_k}\xrightarrow{P}Z\), for each \(j\) choose \(k_j\) increasing with \[P\big(\|Z_{n_{k_j}}-Z\|>2^{-j}\big)<2^{-j}.\] Why possible: for fixed \(\varepsilon=2^{-j}\) the probability tends to 0, so some index makes it \(<2^{-j}\).
  3. Summability \(\Rightarrow\) a.s. \(\sum_j 2^{-j}=1<\infty\), so by Borel–Cantelli (convergent half) the events \(\{\|Z_{n_{k_j}}-Z\|>2^{-j}\}\) occur finitely often a.s. Hence \(\|Z_{n_{k_j}}-Z\|\le2^{-j}\) eventually, i.e. \(Z_{n_{k_j}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}Z\).
  4. Push through \(f\), then integrate. Continuity gives \(f(Z_{n_{k_j}})\xrightarrow{a.s.}f(Z)\); boundedness \(|f|\le B\) lets dominated convergence apply: \[E[f(Z_{n_{k_j}})]\to E[f(Z)].\]
  5. Close the loop. Every subsequence of the numerical sequence \(E[f(Z_n)]\) has a further subsequence converging to \(E f(Z)\); therefore \(E[f(Z_n)]\to E f(Z)\). As \(f\) was arbitrary, \(Z_n\xrightarrow{d}Z\). Note: the final statement is about numbers, not random variables — the original \(Z_n\) need not converge a.s.
Transfers to
\(L^p\Rightarrow\) a.s. along a subsequence (Riesz–Fischer) · \(\xrightarrow{P}\) preserved by continuous maps · consistency proofs in M-estimation · uniqueness of limits in probability.
Pitfall
The typewriter sequence shows the original sequence really can fail to converge a.s. — only the extracted subsequence does.

\(o_P/O_P\) calculus and the transfer ruleConvergenceDef. 1.4, Ex. 1.5

Mechanism. A remainder is usually small relative to the fluctuation \(\|Z_n\|\). The transfer rule converts that into an absolute rate once \(Z_n=O_P(a_n)\) is known. One set inclusion plus a Bonferroni bound does all the work.

a Taylor remainder appears"show this term is negligible"
Full derivation — \(O_P(a_n)\) and \(o_P(\|Z_n\|)\Rightarrow o_P(a_n)\)
  1. Unpack the three statements. \[\lim_{M\to\infty}\limsup_n P\Big(\tfrac{\|Z_n\|}{a_n}>M\Big)=0,\qquad \forall\varepsilon>0:\ P\Big(\tfrac{\|W_n\|}{\|Z_n\|}>\varepsilon\Big)\to0,\] and the goal \(\forall\varepsilon>0:\ P\big(\|W_n\|/a_n>\varepsilon\big)\to0\).
  2. Factor the ratio. On \(\{Z_n\neq0\}\), \[\frac{\|W_n\|}{a_n}=\frac{\|W_n\|}{\|Z_n\|}\cdot\frac{\|Z_n\|}{a_n}.\]
  3. Set inclusion. For any auxiliary \(\varepsilon_1>0\), \[\Big\{\tfrac{\|W_n\|}{\|Z_n\|}<\varepsilon_1\Big\}\cap\Big\{\tfrac{\|Z_n\|}{a_n}<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_1}\Big\}\subseteq\Big\{\tfrac{\|W_n\|}{a_n}<\varepsilon\Big\},\] because on the intersection the product is \(<\varepsilon_1\cdot\varepsilon/\varepsilon_1=\varepsilon\).
  4. Bonferroni. \(P(A\cap B)\ge P(A)+P(B)-1\), which follows from \(P(A\cup B)\le1\) and inclusion–exclusion. So \[P\Big(\tfrac{\|W_n\|}{a_n}<\varepsilon\Big)\ \ge\ P\Big(\tfrac{\|W_n\|}{\|Z_n\|}<\varepsilon_1\Big)+P\Big(\tfrac{\|Z_n\|}{a_n}<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_1}\Big)-1.\]
  5. Choose constants in the right order. Fix \(\delta>0\). First use \(O_P\): pick \(M_0\) with \(\limsup_nP(\|Z_n\|/a_n>M_0)<\delta/2\), and set \(\varepsilon_1:=\varepsilon/M_0\), so \(\varepsilon/\varepsilon_1=M_0\). Then \(\exists L_1\) with, for \(n\ge L_1\), \[P\big(\|Z_n\|/a_n<M_0\big)\ge1-P\big(\|Z_n\|/a_n>M_0\big)>1-\tfrac\delta2.\] Now use \(o_P\) with this frozen \(\varepsilon_1\): \(\exists L_2\) with \(P(\|W_n\|/\|Z_n\|<\varepsilon_1)>1-\delta/2\) for \(n\ge L_2\). Why the order matters: \(\varepsilon_1\) depends on \(M_0\); invoking \(o_P\) first would leave \(\varepsilon_1\) undetermined and the argument circular.
  6. Combine. For \(n\ge\max(L_1,L_2)\), the bound in step 4 gives \(\ge(1-\delta/2)+(1-\delta/2)-1=1-\delta\). Hence \(P(\|W_n\|/a_n\ge\varepsilon)\le\delta\) eventually; \(\varepsilon,\delta\) arbitrary gives \(W_n=o_P(a_n)\).

Immediate consequence used everywhere. If \(\sqrt n(T_n-c)\xrightarrow{d}Z\) then \(T_n-c=O_P(n^{-1/2})\) (Example 1.4), so a Taylor remainder \(R_n=o_P(\|T_n-c\|)\) satisfies \(R_n=o_P(n^{-1/2})\) and \(\sqrt n R_n=o_P(1)\to0\).

Slutsky, CMT, Cramér–WoldConvergenceLem. 1.3, Thm 1.4

Mechanism. Slutsky works because convergence to a constant is equivalent in probability and in distribution; so a two-component vector can be handled by comparing it to a deterministic-second-coordinate version.

studentisingratio or product of estimators
Full derivation — Slutsky in two moves
  1. Compare to the constant version. \[\big\|(Z_n,W_n)-(Z_n,c)\big\|=\|W_n-c\|\xrightarrow{P}0,\] using \(W_n\xrightarrow{d}c\Rightarrow W_n\xrightarrow{P}c\) (Lemma 1.2, part 3 — itself proved by Portmanteau on the closed set \(\{\|x-c\|\ge\varepsilon\}\)).
  2. The constant version converges. For bounded continuous \(f\), the map \(g(x):=f(x,c)\) is bounded continuous, so \[E[f(Z_n,c)]=E[g(Z_n)]\to E[g(Z)]=E[f(Z,c)],\] i.e. \((Z_n,c)\xrightarrow{d}(Z,c)\).
  3. Glue with converging-together. Two sequences that differ by \(o_P(1)\), one of which converges in distribution, have the same limit (Exercise 6). Hence \((Z_n,W_n)\xrightarrow{d}(Z,c)\).
  4. Consequences via CMT. Apply continuous maps \((a,b)\mapsto a+b\), \(ab\), \(a/b\) (the last continuous at \(b=c\neq0\)): \[Z_n+W_n\xrightarrow{d}Z+c,\qquad W_nZ_n\xrightarrow{d}cZ,\qquad Z_n/W_n\xrightarrow{d}Z/c.\]

Worked instance (the \(t\)-statistic). \[\frac{\sqrt n(\bar Z_n-\mu)}{\hat\sigma_n}=\underbrace{\frac{\sqrt n(\bar Z_n-\mu)}{\sigma}}_{\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\sigma}{\hat\sigma_n}}_{\xrightarrow{P}1}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1).\]

Transfers to

Pitfall
The second limit must be constant. Two genuine distributional limits give no joint information — you must prove joint convergence directly (Cramér–Wold).

Borel–Cantelli, both halvesA.s. & tailsLem. 1.1, Cor. 1.1

Mechanism. Convergent half: a non-negative integer-valued sum with finite expectation is a.s. finite. Divergent half: independence turns a sum into a product, and \(1-x\le e^{-x}\) turns the product into an exponential of the divergent sum.

"almost surely" in the conclusionshow a limit theorem fails
Full derivation — both directions and the corollary

Part 1: \(\sum P(A_n)<\infty\Rightarrow P(A_n\text{ i.o.})=0\).

  1. Count occurrences with \(N=\sum_{n\ge1}\mathbf 1_{A_n}\). By monotone convergence (terms \(\ge0\)), \[E[N]=E\Big[\sum_{n=1}^\infty\mathbf 1_{A_n}\Big]=\sum_{n=1}^\infty E[\mathbf 1_{A_n}]=\sum_{n=1}^\infty P(A_n)<\infty.\] Why legal: exchanging \(E\) and \(\sum\) needs non-negativity only — no integrability assumption.
  2. A random variable with finite expectation is a.s. finite, so \(N<\infty\) a.s.: only finitely many \(A_n\) occur, i.e. \(P(A_n\text{ i.o.})=0\).

Part 2: independence and \(\sum P(A_n)=\infty\Rightarrow P(A_n\text{ i.o.})=1\).

  1. For \(m\ge n\), independence of the complements gives \[P\Big(\bigcup_{k=n}^m A_k\Big)=1-\prod_{k=n}^m P(A_k^c)=1-\prod_{k=n}^m\big(1-P(A_k)\big).\]
  2. Apply \(1-x\le e^{-x}\) termwise: \[\prod_{k=n}^m\big(1-P(A_k)\big)\le\exp\Big(-\sum_{k=n}^m P(A_k)\Big)\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}0,\] since the tail of a divergent series of non-negative terms is still \(\infty\).
  3. Continuity from below: \(P(\bigcup_{k\ge n}A_k)=1\) for every \(n\). These sets decrease in \(n\), so continuity from above gives \[P(A_n\text{ i.o.})=P\Big(\bigcap_{n\ge1}\bigcup_{k\ge n}A_k\Big)=\lim_n P\Big(\bigcup_{k\ge n}A_k\Big)=1.\]

Part 3: Corollary 1.1 (a.s. convergence criterion).

  1. Suppose \(\sum_nP(\|Z_n-Z\|>\varepsilon)<\infty\) for every \(\varepsilon>0\). Fix \(m\in\mathbb N\) and apply Part 1 to \(A^{1/m}_n=\{\|Z_n-Z\|>1/m\}\): a.s. only finitely many occur, so \(\exists N_m<\infty\) with \(\|Z_n-Z\|\le1/m\) for all \(n\ge N_m\).
  2. Intersect over \(m\in\mathbb N\) — a countable intersection of probability-one events has probability one. On it, \(\|Z_n-Z\|\to0\). Hence \(Z_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}Z\). The trick: "do it for \(1/m\), then intersect over \(m\)" upgrades a per-\(\varepsilon\) statement to a single a.s. statement. Reused verbatim in Exercise 2 to go from "\(\limsup\ge M\) for each \(M\)" to "\(\limsup=\infty\)".
Transfers to
Rates: choose \(\varepsilon_n\downarrow0\) with \(\sum_nP(|Z_n-Z|>\varepsilon_n)<\infty\) to get \(Z_n-Z=o(\varepsilon_n)\) a.s. · records/extremes dichotomies · the subsequence trick · LIL scaffolding · Kolmogorov 0–1 law companions.
Pitfall
The divergent half needs independence. This is exactly why the Glivenko–Cantelli counterexample \(Z_n=Z_1\) cannot be repaired.

Layer-cake identity and tail integralsA.s. & tails§1.8 throughout

Mechanism. Write \(Y=\int_0^\infty\mathbf 1_{Y>z}\,dz\) and take expectations under Tonelli. It converts a moment into a tail integral, which is the form heavy-tail assumptions arrive in.

you know the tail, not the density"is this moment finite?"
Full derivation — identity, general \(p\), and the Pareto computation
  1. Pointwise identity. For \(Y\ge0\), \(\displaystyle Y(\omega)=\int_0^\infty\mathbf 1_{\{z<Y(\omega)\}}\,dz\) — the length of the interval \([0,Y)\).
  2. Tonelli. The integrand \((\omega,z)\mapsto\mathbf 1_{\{z<Y(\omega)\}}\) is non-negative and jointly measurable, so \[E[Y]=E\!\int_0^\infty\mathbf 1_{\{Y>z\}}dz=\int_0^\infty E[\mathbf 1_{\{Y>z\}}]\,dz=\int_0^\infty P(Y>z)\,dz.\]
  3. General power. Apply the same to \(Y^p\) after substituting \(z=y^p\): \[E[Y^p]=\int_0^\infty P(Y^p>z)\,dz=\int_0^\infty P(Y>y)\,p\,y^{p-1}dy.\]
  4. Pareto, \(P(Y>z)=z^{-\alpha}\) on \([1,\infty)\). The tail is 1 below the support: \[E[Y]=\underbrace{\int_0^1 1\,dz}_{=1}+\int_1^\infty z^{-\alpha}dz =1+\lim_{b\to\infty}\Big[\frac{z^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\Big]_1^b=1+\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{b^{1-\alpha}-1}{1-\alpha}.\] For \(\alpha<1\): \(1-\alpha>0\Rightarrow b^{1-\alpha}\to\infty\Rightarrow E[Y]=\infty\). For \(\alpha>1\): \(b^{1-\alpha}\to0\Rightarrow E[Y]=1+\frac{1}{\alpha-1}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\).
  5. Second moment via the density. Differentiating \(1-z^{-\alpha}\) gives \(f(z)=\alpha z^{-\alpha-1}\), so \[E[Y^2]=\int_1^\infty z^2\alpha z^{-\alpha-1}dz=\alpha\int_1^\infty z^{1-\alpha}dz,\] finite iff \(1-\alpha<-1\), i.e. \(\alpha>2\). Rule to memorise: \(\int_1^\infty z^q\,dz<\infty\iff q<-1\). Every heavy-tail moment question reduces to reading off \(q\).
  6. Truncated tail moment (the Lindeberg input). On \(\{|Y|>a\}\) we have \(Y^2>a^2\), so \[E[Y^2;\,Y>a]\ \ge\ a^2P(Y>a)=a^2\cdot a^{-\alpha}=a^{2-\alpha}\xrightarrow[a\to\infty]{}\infty\quad(\alpha<2).\]
Transfers to

Concentration: Chernoff, Bernstein, and inverting the levelA.s. & tailsThm 1.3

Mechanism. Exponentiate, apply Markov, then optimise over the free parameter. The moment condition \(\frac1n\sum E|\gamma|^m\le\frac{m!}2\kappa^{m-2}\) is designed so the exponential series sums to a geometric expression.

non-asymptotic boundchoose \(n\) for target accuracy
Full derivation — from the cumulant bound to a usable sample size
  1. An elementary cumulant bound. For centred \(X\) and \(c>0\), from \(e^{u}\le\frac{e^{u}}{1+c}\cdot(1+c)\) one gets \[\log E[e^X]\le c:=E[e^{|X|}]-1-E[|X|].\] The point: the right side involves only absolute moments of order \(\ge2\), since \(e^{|x|}-1-|x|=\sum_{m\ge2}|x|^m/m!\).
  2. Insert the moment condition. With \(X_i=\gamma(Z_i)/L\) and \(S_n=\sum_i\gamma(Z_i)\), \[\log E\big[e^{S_n/L}\big]\le\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{m\ge2}\frac{E|\gamma(Z_i)|^m}{L^m m!} \le\sum_{m\ge2}\frac{1}{L^mm!}\cdot n\frac{m!}{2}\kappa^{m-2} =\frac{n}{2L^2}\sum_{m\ge2}\Big(\frac{\kappa}{L}\Big)^{m-2}.\]
  3. Sum the geometric series (requires \(L>\kappa\)): \[\sum_{m\ge2}(\kappa/L)^{m-2}=\frac{1}{1-\kappa/L}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\log E[e^{S_n/L}]\le\frac{n}{2L^2(1-\kappa/L)}=\frac{n}{2(L^2-L\kappa)}.\]
  4. Markov on the exponential (Chernoff). \[P(S_n\ge a)=P\big(e^{S_n/L}\ge e^{a/L}\big)\le e^{-a/L}E[e^{S_n/L}]\le\exp\Big(-\frac aL+\frac{n}{2(L^2-L\kappa)}\Big).\]
  5. Optimise over \(L\). Taking \(\kappa/L=1-(1+2a\kappa/n)^{-1/2}\) and then writing \(a/n=\kappa t+\sqrt{2t}\) collapses the exponent to \(-nt\): \[P\Big(\tfrac1n\sum_i\gamma(Z_i)\ge\kappa t+\sqrt{2t}\Big)\le e^{-nt}.\]
  6. Symmetrise. \(-\gamma\) satisfies the same hypotheses with the same \(\kappa\) (since \(|-\gamma|^m=|\gamma|^m\)), so the union bound gives the factor 2: \[P\big(|\bar\gamma_n|\ge\kappa t+\sqrt{2t}\big)\le2e^{-nt}.\]
  7. Invert the level. Set \(\kappa t+\sqrt{2t}=\varepsilon\) and substitute \(s=\sqrt{2t}\) (so \(t=s^2/2\)), then \(x=\kappa s\): \[\varepsilon=\kappa\tfrac{s^2}{2}+s=s\big(1+\tfrac x2\big),\quad 1+\kappa\varepsilon=1+x+\tfrac{x^2}{2},\quad \varepsilon^2=s^2\big(1+\tfrac x2\big)^2.\] Therefore \[\frac{t(1+\kappa\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^2}=\frac{\tfrac{s^2}{2}\big(1+x+\tfrac{x^2}{2}\big)}{s^2\big(1+x+\tfrac{x^2}{4}\big)}=\frac{\tfrac12\big(1+x+\tfrac{x^2}{2}\big)}{1+x+\tfrac{x^2}{4}}\ \ge\ \frac12,\] the last step because \(\tfrac{x^2}{2}\ge\tfrac{x^2}{4}\). Hence \(t\ge\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2(1+\kappa\varepsilon)}\) and \[P(|\bar\gamma_n|\ge\varepsilon)\le2\exp\Big(-\frac{n\varepsilon^2}{2(1+\kappa\varepsilon)}\Big).\]
  8. Solve for the sample size. Requiring the bound \(\le\eta\): \[2e^{-n\varepsilon^2/(2(1+\kappa\varepsilon))}\le\eta\iff n\ \ge\ \frac{2(1+\kappa\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^2}\log\frac2\eta.\]

Two regimes. Small \(\varepsilon\): denominator \(\approx1\), exponent \(\approx-n\varepsilon^2/2\) (sub-Gaussian). Large \(\varepsilon\): denominator \(\approx\kappa\varepsilon\), exponent \(\approx-n\varepsilon/(2\kappa)\) (sub-exponential).

Fourth-moment SLLN: the expansion in fullLLN/CLTEx. 1.1

Mechanism. Under independence and mean zero, only index patterns where every index appears at least twice survive in \(E(\sum Z_i)^4\). Counting them gives the \(n^2\) rate that makes the Borel–Cantelli sum converge.

you may assume high momentswant a.s. cheaply
Full derivation — why \(nEZ^4+3n(n-1)(EZ^2)^2\)
  1. Expand the fourth power. \[E\Big(\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i\Big)^4=\sum_{i,j,k,l}E[Z_iZ_jZ_kZ_l].\]
  2. Kill terms with a lone index. If some index (say \(i\)) differs from all others, independence factorises the expectation and \(E[Z_i]=0\) makes the whole term vanish. So only patterns where each distinct index appears \(\ge2\) times contribute.
  3. Enumerate surviving patterns. With four slots, the possibilities are:
    • all four equal, \(i=j=k=l\): \(n\) terms, each \(E[Z_1^4]\);
    • two distinct values each appearing twice: choose the pair of values (\(n(n-1)\) ordered) and choose which 2 of the 4 slots carry the first value — \(\binom42/…\) giving the multiplicity \(3\) per ordered pair. Each term is \(E[Z_1^2]^2\).
    Hence \[E\Big(\sum_iZ_i\Big)^4=nE[Z_1^4]+3n(n-1)\big(E[Z_1^2]\big)^2.\]
  4. Normalise. With \(S_n=\frac1n\sum_iZ_i\), \[E[S_n^4]=\frac{nE[Z_1^4]+3n(n-1)(E[Z_1^2])^2}{n^4}\le\frac{C}{n^2},\qquad C:=E[Z_1^4]+3(E[Z_1^2])^2.\] Leading behaviour is \(3(EZ_1^2)^2/n^2\): the \(n^2\) pairings beat the \(n\) diagonal terms.
  5. Markov at the fourth power. \(P(|S_n|>\varepsilon)\le E[S_n^4]/\varepsilon^4\le C/(n^2\varepsilon^4)\), so \(S_n\xrightarrow{P}0\).
  6. Summability and Borel–Cantelli. \(\sum_n n^{-2}=\pi^2/6<\infty\), so Corollary 1.1 upgrades to \(S_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}0\). Why the fourth and not the second moment: Chebyshev gives only \(C/(n\varepsilon^2)\), and \(\sum1/n=\infty\) — not summable. The fourth moment is the cheapest power that makes the series converge.
Transfers to
Complete convergence results · U-statistics variance computations (same "which indices coincide" bookkeeping) · method of moments proofs of limit laws · Wick/Isserlis formulas in the Gaussian case.

CLT via characteristic functionsLLN/CLTThm 1.6

Mechanism. Two derivatives of \(\varphi\) exist exactly when \(E\xi^2<\infty\); Taylor to second order and the limit \((1-c/n+o(1/n))^n\to e^{-c}\) do the rest. Cramér–Wold lifts it to \(\mathbb R^p\).

iid, finite second moment
Full derivation — including the differentiation-under-the-integral step
  1. Project to one dimension. Fix \(a\in\mathbb R^p\), set \(\xi_k=a^\top(Z_k-\mu)\). Then \(E\xi_1=0\) and \(E\xi_1^2=a^\top\mathrm{Var}(Z_1)a<\infty\).
  2. Differentiate \(\varphi(t)=E[e^{it\xi_1}]\) twice. The difference quotient of \(e^{it\xi}\) is bounded by \(|\xi|\) using \(|e^{ih}-1|\le|h|\); since \(E|\xi_1|\le(E\xi_1^2)^{1/2}<\infty\), dominated convergence justifies \[\varphi'(t)=E[i\xi_1e^{it\xi_1}],\qquad \varphi''(t)=E[(i\xi_1)^2e^{it\xi_1}].\]
  3. Evaluate at 0. \(\varphi(0)=1\), \(\varphi'(0)=iE[\xi_1]=0\), \(\varphi''(0)=-E[\xi_1^2]\). Taylor with Peano remainder: \[\varphi(t)=1-\frac{t^2}{2}E[\xi_1^2]+o(t^2),\qquad t\to0.\]
  4. Characteristic function of the normalised sum. By independence, \[E\Big[\exp\Big(\tfrac{it}{\sqrt n}\sum_{k\le n}\xi_k\Big)\Big]=\varphi\Big(\frac{t}{\sqrt n}\Big)^{\!n} =\Big(1-\frac{t^2}{2n}E[\xi_1^2]+o(n^{-1})\Big)^{\!n}.\] Why \(o(n^{-1})\): substituting \(t/\sqrt n\) into \(o(t^2)\) gives \(o(t^2/n)\), and \(t\) is fixed.
  5. Take the limit. Using \((1+c_n/n)^n\to e^c\) whenever \(c_n\to c\), \[\varphi(t/\sqrt n)^n\longrightarrow\exp\Big(-\frac{t^2}{2}E[\xi_1^2]\Big)=\exp\Big(-\frac{t^2}{2}a^\top\mathrm{Var}(Z_1)a\Big),\] the characteristic function of \(N(0,a^\top\mathrm{Var}(Z_1)a)\).
  6. Cramér–Wold. Since this holds for every \(a\), \(\sqrt n(\bar Z_n-\mu)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\mathrm{Var}(Z_1))\).

Mechanism. Replace the array entries one at a time by matched Gaussians. A telescoping product bound converts "the products differ" into "the individual characteristic functions differ", and each difference is controlled by a third-order Taylor remainder that the Lindeberg condition kills.

Full derivation — the four inequalities that carry the proof
  1. Reduce to one dimension and set notation. \(X_{nm}=a^\top Z_{nm}\), \(c_{nm}=E[X_{nm}^2]\), \(c_n=\sum_mc_{nm}\to a^\top\Sigma a\). Introduce independent \(\zeta_{nm}\sim N(0,c_{nm})\), so \(\sum_m\zeta_{nm}\sim N(0,c_n)\) exactly.
  2. Uniform smallness of row variances. For any \(\varepsilon>0\), \[\sup_m c_{nm}\le\varepsilon^2+\sup_mE[X_{nm}^2;|X_{nm}|>\varepsilon]\le\varepsilon^2+\sum_mE[X_{nm}^2;|X_{nm}|>\varepsilon]\to\varepsilon^2,\] so \(\sup_mc_{nm}\to0\) by letting \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\). Splitting a second moment at a threshold — small part bounded by \(\varepsilon^2\), large part is the Lindeberg sum — is the recurring move.
  3. Telescoping product bound. For complex numbers with \(|z_m|,|z'_m|\le1\), \[\Big|\prod_{m=1}^nz_m-\prod_{m=1}^nz'_m\Big|\le\sum_{m=1}^n|z_m-z'_m|,\] proved by induction (write \(\prod z-\prod z'=\prod' (z_1-z_1')+z_1'(\prod_{m\ge2}z-\prod_{m\ge2}z')\)). This is what makes "replace one factor at a time" rigorous.
  4. Complex-exponential remainder. With \(h_m(x)=e^{ix}-\sum_{k=0}^m(ix)^k/k!\), the recursion \(h_m(x)=i\int_0^xh_{m-1}(s)ds\) together with \(|h_{-1}|\equiv1\), \(|h_0|\le2\) gives by induction \[\Big|e^{ix}-\sum_{k=0}^m\frac{(ix)^k}{k!}\Big|\le\frac{2|x|^m}{m!}\wedge\frac{|x|^{m+1}}{(m+1)!}.\] With \(m=2\): \(\big|e^{itx}-1-itx+\tfrac12t^2x^2\big|\le|tx|^2(1\wedge|tx|)\). Why the minimum matters: the \(|x|^3\) branch is used for small \(x\), the \(|x|^2\) branch for large \(x\) — exactly the split Lindeberg controls.
  5. Apply to both families. Taking expectations (\(E X_{nm}=0\), \(EX_{nm}^2=c_{nm}\)): \[\big|\varphi_{nm}(t)-1+\tfrac12t^2c_{nm}\big|\le t^2E\big[X_{nm}^2(1\wedge|tX_{nm}|)\big],\] and identically for \(\psi_{nm}(t)=e^{-t^2c_{nm}/2}\) with \(\zeta_{nm}\) in place of \(X_{nm}\).
  6. Combine 3 and 5. \[\Big|\prod_m\varphi_{nm}-\prod_m\psi_{nm}\Big|\le t^2\sum_mE\big[X_{nm}^2(1\wedge|tX_{nm}|)\big]+t^2\sum_mE\big[\zeta_{nm}^2(1\wedge|t\zeta_{nm}|)\big].\]
  7. Kill the first sum with Lindeberg. Split at \(\varepsilon\): \[\sum_mE\big[X_{nm}^2(1\wedge|tX_{nm}|)\big]\le\varepsilon|t|\sum_mc_{nm}+\sum_mE\big[X_{nm}^2;|X_{nm}|>\varepsilon\big]\to\varepsilon|t|\,a^\top\Sigma a,\] then \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\).
  8. Kill the second sum with the third moment. Using \(1\wedge u\le u\) and \(E|\zeta_{nm}|^3=E|\xi|^3c_{nm}^{3/2}\), \[\sum_mE\big[\zeta_{nm}^2(1\wedge|t\zeta_{nm}|)\big]\le|t|^3E|\xi|^3\sum_mc_{nm}^{3/2}\le|t|^3E|\xi|^3\,c_n\sup_mc_{nm}^{1/2}\to0\] by step 2. Hence the characteristic functions agree in the limit, and \(\sum_mZ_{nm}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\Sigma)\) by Cramér–Wold.

Lyapounov shortcut. On \(\{\|Z_{nm}\|>c\}\), \(\|Z_{nm}\|^2\le c^{2-\alpha}\|Z_{nm}\|^\alpha\) for \(\alpha>2\), so \(\sum_mE[\|Z_{nm}\|^2;\|Z_{nm}\|>c]\le c^{2-\alpha}\sum_mE\|Z_{nm}\|^\alpha\to0\).

Full derivation — the proof, then the sample-variance computation line by line

The proof (Thm 1.10).

  1. \(\nabla h(c)^\top\) is linear and continuous, so by CMT applied to \(a_n(T_n-c)\xrightarrow{d}Z\): \(\nabla h(c)^\top a_n(T_n-c)\xrightarrow{d}\nabla h(c)^\top Z\).

  3. Differentiability at \(c\) means \(h(T_n)-h(c)=\nabla h(c)^\top(T_n-c)+o(\|T_n-c\|)\), and the transfer rule converts \(o(\|T_n-c\|)\) into \(o_P(a_n^{-1})\).
  4. Multiply by \(a_n\): \(a_n(h(T_n)-h(c))=\nabla h(c)^\top a_n(T_n-c)+o_P(1)\), and converging-together (Exercise 6) gives the limit \(\nabla h(c)^\top Z\).

Worked: sample variance, all steps.

  1. Write it as a smooth function of averages. \[\hat\sigma_n^2=\frac1n\sum_i(Z_i-\bar Z_n)^2=\frac1n\sum_iZ_i^2-\bar Z_n^2=h(T_n),\quad T_n=\Big(\bar Z_n,\ \tfrac1n\sum_iZ_i^2\Big)^\top,\ h(x,y)=y-x^2.\]
  2. CLT for the pair. Laplace applied to the iid vectors \((Z_i,Z_i^2)^\top\) gives \(\sqrt n(T_n-(\mu_1,\mu_2)^\top)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\Sigma)\) with \[\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\mu_2-\mu_1^2&\mu_3-\mu_1\mu_2\\ \mu_3-\mu_1\mu_2&\mu_4-\mu_2^2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mu_k=E[Z_1^k].\]
  3. Gradient. \(\nabla h(x,y)=(-2x,1)^\top\).
  4. Sandwich (centred case \(\mu_1=0\), so \(\mu_2=\mu^{(2)}\), \(\mu_4=\mu^{(4)}\)). Then \(\nabla h=(0,1)^\top\), which simply selects \(\Sigma_{22}\): \[\nabla h^\top\Sigma\nabla h=\Sigma_{22}=\mu_4-\mu_2^2=\mu^{(4)}-(\mu^{(2)})^2.\] In the general (non-centred) case the same computation gives \((-2\mu_1)^2\Sigma_{11}+2(-2\mu_1)\Sigma_{12}+\Sigma_{22}\), which reduces to \(\mu^{(4)}-(\mu^{(2)})^2\) in central moments.
  5. Conclusion. \(\sqrt n(\hat\sigma_n^2-\mu^{(2)})\xrightarrow{d}N\big(0,\mu^{(4)}-(\mu^{(2)})^2\big)\).

Variance stabilisation (Fisher's \(z\)). From \(\sqrt n(r_n-\rho)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,(1-\rho^2)^2)\), choose \(h\) with \(h'(\rho)=1/(1-\rho^2)\), i.e. \(h(r)=\tfrac12\log\frac{1+r}{1-r}\). Then the sandwich gives \(h'(\rho)^2(1-\rho^2)^2=1\): the limit is \(N(0,1)\), free of \(\rho\).

Degenerate case. If \(\nabla h(c)=0\), expand to second order: \(n(h(T_n)-h(c))\xrightarrow{d}\tfrac12Z^\top\nabla^2h(c)Z\), a quadratic form (\(\chi^2\)-type), not normal.

Partition-then-sup (pointwise ⟶ uniform)ProcessesLem. 1.5, Thm 1.15

Mechanism. Monotonicity lets a value at an arbitrary \(x\) be sandwiched between values at neighbouring grid points. Boundedness of the limit lets the grid be chosen so the limit moves by \(\le\varepsilon\) per cell — so the whole supremum costs only \(\max\) over finitely many points plus \(\varepsilon\).

upgrade pointwise to uniformmonotone / càdlàg object
Full derivation — the sandwich, written out
  1. Choose the grid. Given \(\varepsilon>0\), pick \(-\infty=x_0<x_1<\dots<x_K=\infty\) with \[T(x_j-)-T(x_{j-1})<\varepsilon\qquad (j=1,\dots,K).\] Why possible: \(T\) is non-decreasing and bounded, so its total increase is finite; include any jumps larger than \(\varepsilon\) as grid points, and subdivide the rest.
  2. Grid error. Define \(\Delta^{(n)}=\max_{j=1,\dots,K-1}\big\{|T^{(n)}(x_j)-T(x_j)|,\ |T^{(n)}(x_j-)-T(x_j-)|\big\}\). By hypothesis each entry \(\xrightarrow{a.s.}0\), and a maximum of finitely many null sequences is null: \(\Delta^{(n)}\xrightarrow{a.s.}0\).
  3. Upper sandwich. For arbitrary \(x\), pick \(j\) with \(x\in[x_{j-1},x_j)\). Monotonicity of \(T^{(n)}\) and of \(T\) gives \[T^{(n)}(x)-T(x)\ \le\ T^{(n)}(x_j-)-T(x_{j-1})\ =\ \underbrace{\big[T^{(n)}(x_j-)-T(x_j-)\big]}_{\le\Delta^{(n)}}+\underbrace{\big[T(x_j-)-T(x_{j-1})\big]}_{<\varepsilon}.\]
  4. Lower sandwich. Symmetrically, \[T^{(n)}(x)-T(x)\ \ge\ T^{(n)}(x_{j-1})-T(x_j-)=\big[T^{(n)}(x_{j-1})-T(x_{j-1})\big]-\big[T(x_j-)-T(x_{j-1})\big]\ \ge\ -\Delta^{(n)}-\varepsilon.\]
  5. Take the supremum. \(x\) was arbitrary, so \[\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}\big|T^{(n)}(x)-T(x)\big|\ \le\ \Delta^{(n)}+\varepsilon.\]
  6. Let \(n\to\infty\), then \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\). \(\limsup_n\sup_x|T^{(n)}-T|\le\varepsilon\) a.s. for each \(\varepsilon\); intersecting over \(\varepsilon=1/m\) gives \(\sup_x|T^{(n)}-T|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0\).

Glivenko–Cantelli follows. Take \(T^{(n)}=F^{(n)}\), \(T=F\): pointwise a.s. convergence at \(x\) and \(x-\) is the SLLN applied to \(\mathbf 1_{Z_i\le x}\) and \(\mathbf 1_{Z_i<x}\).

Dini variant (Thm 1.15). Same grid argument for monotone processes converging in probability to a continuous \(f\): choose the partition so \(f(s_i)-f(s_{i-1})\le\eta/2\), then \(\sup_{s\in[a,b]}|X_n(s)-f(s)|\le\max_{k\le K}|X_n(s_k)-f(s_k)|+\eta/2\), and the finitely many terms vanish in probability.

Transfers to

Pitfall
Only the limit must be bounded; the approximants need not be. That asymmetry is what lets the lemma cover the unbounded cumulative hazard.

Bracketing numbers and the entropy integralProcessesDefs 1.16, Thms 1.18–1.19

Mechanism. Cover the class by \(\varepsilon\)-brackets; a finite \(L^1\) count gives a uniform LLN, a finite \(L^2\) entropy integral gives a functional CLT. For indicator half-lines the count is \(O(\varepsilon^{-2})\), and \(\sqrt{\log(\cdot)}\) is integrable.

Full derivation — brackets for half-lines and the integral computation
  1. The class. \(\mathcal F=\{f_t=\mathbf 1_{[0,t]}:t\in[0,\infty)\}\), so that \(\mathbb G^{(n)}f_t=\sqrt n(F^{(n)}(t)-F(t))\).
  2. Construct brackets. Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(0=t_0<t_1<\dots<t_k=\infty\) with \[F(t_j-)-F(t_{j-1})<\varepsilon^2,\qquad j=1,\dots,k,\] possible since \(F\) is non-decreasing, càdlàg and bounded by 1. Set \(l_j=\mathbf 1_{[0,t_{j-1})}\), \(u_j=\mathbf 1_{[0,t_j)}\).
  3. Check the bracket covers. For \(t\in[t_{j-1},t_j)\), \(l_j\le\mathbf 1_{[0,t]}\le u_j\) pointwise.
  4. Measure the bracket in \(L^2\). Since \(u_j-l_j=\mathbf 1_{[t_{j-1},t_j)}\) is an indicator, \((u_j-l_j)^2=u_j-l_j\), so \[\|u_j-l_j\|_{L^2(P)}=\big(E[u_j-l_j]\big)^{1/2}=\big(F(t_j-)-F(t_{j-1})\big)^{1/2}<\varepsilon.\] This is why the spacing is \(\varepsilon^2\): the \(L^2\) length is the square root of the probability gap.
  5. Count. Total increase of \(F\) is 1 and each cell uses up \(\approx\varepsilon^2\), so \(k\le\lceil2/\varepsilon^2\rceil\), i.e. \[N_{[\,]}\big(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L^2(P)\big)\le\lceil2/\varepsilon^2\rceil.\]
  6. Entropy integral converges. \[\int_{(0,1)}\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)}\,d\varepsilon\le\int_0^1\sqrt{\log\big(2/\varepsilon^2+1\big)}\,d\varepsilon<\infty.\] Why finite: \(\log(2/\varepsilon^2+1)\le C+2\log(1/\varepsilon)\) for small \(\varepsilon\), and substituting \(u=\log(1/\varepsilon)\), \(\varepsilon=e^{-u}\), \(d\varepsilon=-e^{-u}du\), \[\int_0^1\sqrt{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\,d\varepsilon=\int_0^\infty\sqrt u\,e^{-u}du=\Gamma(3/2)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}<\infty.\] The moral: polynomial bracketing numbers \(\varepsilon^{-p}\) always give a convergent entropy integral, since \(\sqrt{\log}\) grows too slowly to matter.
  7. Conclusion. \(\mathcal F\) is \(P\)-Donsker, so \(\sqrt n(F^{(n)}-F)\xrightarrow{d}\mathbb G\) in \(\ell^\infty\), and matching covariances identifies \(\mathbb G=B\circ F\).

Other classes, same recipe. Parametric \(\{f_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb R^d\}\) with \(|f_{\theta_1}-f_{\theta_2}|\le m\|\theta_1-\theta_2\|\) gives \(N_{[\,]}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-d}\); Hölder balls of smoothness \(s\) on \([0,1]^d\) give \(\log N_{[\,]}\asymp\varepsilon^{-d/s}\), Donsker iff \(s>d/2\).

Full derivation — the theorem, the ratio map, and the inverse map

1. Why the theorem is a two-line consequence of CMT.

  1. Define \(g_n(h)=r_n\big(\phi(\theta+r_n^{-1}h)-\phi(\theta)\big)\) on \(\mathbb D_n=\{h:\theta+r_n^{-1}h\in\mathbb D_\phi\}\).
  2. Hadamard differentiability says precisely: \(h_n\to h\in\mathbb D_0\Rightarrow g_n(h_n)\to\phi'_\theta(h)\) (take \(t_n=r_n^{-1}\)).
  3. The extended CMT (Thm 1.14) then gives, from \(r_n(X_n-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}X\), \[r_n\big(\phi(X_n)-\phi(\theta)\big)=g_n\big(r_n(X_n-\theta)\big)\xrightarrow{d}\phi'_\theta(X).\] Why Gâteaux is not enough: it only allows the fixed direction \(h_n\equiv h\), but \(r_n(X_n-\theta)\) is a genuinely varying random element. Why not Fréchet: the inverse map is not Fréchet differentiable in sup-norm.

2. The ratio map \(\phi(f,g)=f/g\). With \(u_n\to0\) and \((h_{1n},h_{2n})\to(h_1,h_2)\):

\[\frac{\phi\big((f,g)+u_n(h_{1n},h_{2n})\big)-\phi(f,g)}{u_n} =\frac{1}{u_n}\left[\frac{f+u_nh_{1n}}{g+u_nh_{2n}}-\frac fg\right] =\frac{(f+u_nh_{1n})g-f(g+u_nh_{2n})}{u_n\,g\,(g+u_nh_{2n})}\] \[=\frac{u_n\big(h_{1n}g-fh_{2n}\big)}{u_n\,g\,(g+u_nh_{2n})} =\frac{h_{1n}g-fh_{2n}}{g\,(g+u_nh_{2n})}\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{h_1g-fh_2}{g^2}=\frac{h_1}{g}-\frac{f h_2}{g^2}.\]

Conditions used: \(u_nh_{2n}\to0\) uniformly and \(\inf|g|>0\) on a compact index set, so the denominator stays bounded away from 0 and the convergence is in sup-norm. Note the answer is the same shape as \(\nabla(x/y)=(1/y,-x/y^2)\).

3. The inverse map (quantiles), pointwise expansion. Let \(q_u=F^{-1}(u)\), \(q_{u,n}=(F+t_nh_n)^{-1}(u)\).

  1. By definition of the generalised inverse, for small \(\epsilon_n=o(t_n)\), \[(F+t_nh_n)(q_{u,n}-\epsilon_n)\ \le\ u\ \le\ (F+t_nh_n)(q_{u,n}).\]

Full derivation — inversion, rejection, and the Fourier envelope constants

1. The equivalence and inversion.

  1. \(F^{-1}(u)=\inf\{y:F(y)\ge u\}\). Right-continuity of \(F\) makes \(\{y:F(y)\ge u\}\) a closed half-line \([F^{-1}(u),\infty)\), whence \[u\le F(z)\iff F^{-1}(u)\le z.\]
  2. Therefore \(P(F^{-1}(U)\le z)=P(U\le F(z))=F(z)\): inversion sampling works with no continuity assumption.
  3. Conversely, if \(F\) is continuous, \(P(F(Z)\ge u)=P(Z\ge F^{-1}(u))=1-F(F^{-1}(u))=1-u\), using \(F(F^{-1}(u))=u\) (which needs continuity). So \(F(Z)\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\).

2. Acceptance–rejection: why the accepted draw has density \(f\).

  1. Acceptance probability, conditioning on \(Y_1\): \[p=P(A_1)=E\Big[\frac{f(Y_1)}{Cg(Y_1)}\Big]=\int\frac{f(y)}{Cg(y)}g(y)\,dy=\frac1C\int f=\frac1C.\]
  2. Joint event on a set \(B\): \[P(A_1,Y_1\in B)=\int_Bg(y)\frac{f(y)}{Cg(y)}dy=\frac1C\int_Bf=p\int_Bf.\]
  3. Sum over the geometric trial index (independence across trials): \[P(Z\in B)=\sum_{n\ge1}(1-p)^{n-1}P(A_1,Y_1\in B)=p\int_Bf\cdot\frac{1}{1-(1-p)}=\int_Bf.\] Expected number of proposals \(=1/p=C\).

3. Fourier envelope: where \(b=\sqrt{k/c}\) and \(A=4\sqrt{kc}\) come from.

  1. From \(f(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\psi(s)e^{-isz}ds\), the trivial bound is \(|f(z)|\le\frac{1}{2\pi}\int|\psi|=:c\).
  2. Integrating by parts twice (legal since \(\psi,\psi',\psi''\in L^1\Rightarrow\psi,\psi'\to0\)): \[f(z)=-\frac{1}{2\pi z^2}\int\psi''(s)e^{-isz}ds\ \Longrightarrow\ |f(z)|\le\frac{k}{z^2},\quad k:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int|\psi''|.\]
  3. So \(f\le c\wedge kz^{-2}\). The two branches cross where \(c=k/z^2\), i.e. at \(z=\pm b\) with \(b=\sqrt{k/c}\).
  4. Total mass of the envelope: \[A=\int_{-\infty}^\infty(c\wedge kz^{-2})dz=2\int_0^bc\,dz+2\int_b^\infty kz^{-2}dz=2cb+\frac{2k}{b} =2c\sqrt{k/c}+2k\sqrt{c/k}=4\sqrt{kc}.\] Hence \(g=(c\mathbf 1_{|z|\le b}+kz^{-2}\mathbf 1_{|z|>b})/A\) is a density and \(f\le Ag\).

Instances
Converging-together: \(Ef(W_n)-Ef(Z)=[Ef(W_n)-Ef(Z_n)]+[Ef(Z_n)-Ef(Z)]\).

Worked example — the move in action

Learn the chain, not the prose. In an exam you reconstruct an argument by recalling which move comes next and why the previous one made it possible.

Strong law under a fourth moment

\(EZ_1=0\), \(EZ_1^4<\infty\Rightarrow\bar S_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}0\), with no maximal inequality.

  2. Divide by \(n^4\): \(E[\bar S_n^4]\le C/n^2\). Markov: \(P(|\bar S_n|>\varepsilon)\le C/(n^2\varepsilon^4)\).

Chebyshev would give \(C/(n\varepsilon^2)\) and \(\sum1/n=\infty\) — not summable. The fourth moment is the cheapest power that makes Borel–Cantelli fire.

Glivenko–Cantelli

  2. Grid: \(F(x_j-)-F(x_{j-1})<\varepsilon\), possible since \(F\) is bounded and monotone (put jumps on the grid).
  3. Sandwich: \(|F^{(n)}(x)-F(x)|\le\Delta^{(n)}+\varepsilon\) for all \(x\), with \(\Delta^{(n)}\) a max of finitely many null sequences.
  4. \(\varepsilon=1/m\), intersect over \(m\).
Two ingredients only: pointwise convergence and monotonicity. Kill independence and the pointwise step dies (\(Z_n=Z_1\) keeps the sup at \(\ge\frac12\)); kill monotonicity and the sandwich has nothing to grip.

The generic ratio-estimator skeleton
