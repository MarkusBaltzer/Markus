Decode the question, then the proof writes itself

An exam question never tells you the class, the grid, or the fineness. It tells you five things, all in plain sight, and those five things determine everything else. Answer them in the panel below and watch the entire setup assemble — mechanism, currency, cage, budget, count, and the sentence you finish with.

Q1Is the object an average over the sample?

Write the statistic as $\tfrac1n\sum_i(\cdot)$. If you can, what is inside is $f$. If you cannot, it is a smooth function of something that is.

Q2At fixed $z$, is $\theta\mapsto f_\theta(z)$ monotone?

This is the only question that selects the cage. Test it by moving $\theta$ with $z$ held still.

Q3Write the gap $u-l$. What kind of object is it?

This is the question that decides $\varepsilon^2$ versus $\varepsilon$. Nothing else decides it.

Q4How many numbers is the parameter?

This is the exponent of the count, and the number of ways the budget splits.

Q5Is the parameter range bounded?

Only matters in the length currency. In the mass currency the extent is $1$ whatever the range.

What the question is actually telling you

The exam question is a specification, written in code

A question like “let $Z_1,\dots,Z_n$ be iid with $\E[Z_1^2]<\infty$ and let $\mathcal F=\{z\mapsto e^{-\lambda z}:\lambda\in[0,1]\}$; show $\mathcal F$ is Donsker” looks like it is handing you a class and asking for work. It is doing more than that. Every clause in it is a constraint the examiner had to write down in order for the answer to exist, and each one pins a row of the decoder.

Read that sentence again, clause by clause. $\E[Z_1^2]<\infty$ says the envelope will involve $z$, so you are in the length currency and $\|Z_1\|_{L_2}$ will appear in your count. $\lambda\in[0,1]$ says the range is bounded — which it has to be, because in the length currency the range divides. $e^{-\lambda z}$ with $z\ge0$ is decreasing in $\lambda$, so the cage is exact and you never need a Lipschitz pad. Three clauses, three rows of the setup, before you have written anything.

The reading habit to build

Assumptions in these questions are not hygiene. They are the examiner telling you which case you are in, because without them the proof they have in mind does not close. Every hypothesis you are given is load-bearing somewhere in the count. If you finish an answer without having used one of them, you have almost certainly taken the wrong route.

Phrase book

Clause by clause, what to write down

These are the phrases that actually recur, and what each one settles. Learn the right-hand column; the left-hand column is what you will be shown.

“Prove Lemma A by applying Lemma B”An assembly question — Slutsky, continuous mapping, marginals to joints. No brackets at all. Do not start gridding.

The one misreading that costs most

Seeing a two-parameter class and building one grid indexed by a single $j$. A pair $(s,t)$ with $s$ in cell $3$ and $t$ in cell $7$ then lies in no bracket at all, and the cover is not a cover. Two parameters, two indices. The count squares, the budget halves, and nothing else changes.

Before you think

The skeleton, written from memory in ninety seconds

Write this down first, with blanks, before working anything out. It earns structure marks on its own, it stops you forgetting the finite-dimensional half, and it tells you exactly which quantity you still have to compute.

Copy this shape

1. The class. “Let $f_\theta(z)=\underline{\phantom{xx}}$ and $\mathcal F=\{f_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}$. Then $\sqrt n(\widehat\cdot-\cdot)(\theta)=\Gn f_\theta$, so it suffices to show $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Donsker.”

2. Finite-dimensional distributions. “Fix $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_M$. The vectors $(f_{\theta_r}(Z_i))_{r\le M}$ are iid with finite second moments, so by the multivariate CLT $\Gn$ converges on these coordinates to a centred Gaussian with $\operatorname{Cov}=Pf_{\theta_r}f_{\theta_s}-Pf_{\theta_r}Pf_{\theta_s}=\underline{\phantom{xx}}$.”

3. The brackets. “Let $\varepsilon>0$ be given. Choose $\underline{\phantom{xx}}$ such that $\underline{\phantom{xx}}\le\underline{\phantom{xx}}$, possible because $\underline{\phantom{xx}}$. Define $l_j=\underline{\phantom{xx}}$, $u_j=\underline{\phantom{xx}}$. For $\theta$ in cell $j$ and every $z$, $l_j(z)\le f_\theta(z)\le u_j(z)$, because $\underline{\phantom{xx}}$.”

4. The size and the count. “$\|u_j-l_j\|_{L_2(P)}=\underline{\phantom{xx}}\le\varepsilon$, hence $\Nb(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2(P))\le\underline{\phantom{xx}}$.”

5. The invariant sentence. “This is polynomial in $1/\varepsilon$, so $\int_0^1\sqrt{\log \Nb(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2(P))}\,d\varepsilon<\infty$, and by the bracketing CLT $\mathcal F$ is Donsker. Therefore $\sqrt n(\widehat\cdot-\cdot)\rightsquigarrow\mathbb G$ in $\linf(\Theta)$, where $\mathbb G$ is the Gaussian process of step 2.”

Steps 1, 2 and 5 are the same in every question and can be written before you understand the class. Step 4 is arithmetic once step 3 is done. All the thinking lives in the two blanks of step 3 — what you cut, and how finely — and the decoder above fills both.

Say it in this order, out loud

Class. Margins. Cage. Size. Count. Sentence. If you can recite those six words you can reconstruct the skeleton under pressure, and the examiner can see the shape of a complete answer from the first line.

Gap lab

Move the cell width and watch which currency you are in

The gap is the only object in the proof you have to compute, and everything downstream is read off it. Here it is, drawn. Pick a class, widen the cell, and watch two numbers: the width $\delta$ you chose, and the $L_2$ size of the shaded region. The relationship between those two numbers is the currency.

Why two numbers are shown

The proof never computes the exact $L_2$ size of the gap. It computes an upper bound — $\sqrt{\text{mass}}$, or $\delta\|m\|_{L_2}$ from the Lipschitz step — and requires that to be at most $\varepsilon$. The exact norm is smaller, sometimes much smaller, and the difference is slack you are entitled to. Never try to close that gap: only $\log\Nb$ matters, so sharpening a constant buys nothing and costs minutes.

What to notice in the first class

Halve $\delta$ and the shaded area halves, but its $L_2$ size only falls by $\sqrt2$. That is the whole reason indicator classes cost $\varepsilon^2$: to buy a factor of two in the norm you must pay a factor of four in the cell. The square root is not an artefact of the algebra — you can watch it happen.

What to notice in the other three

Halve $\delta$ and the norm halves exactly. Nothing collapses, because the gap is a genuine function rather than a set. So the budget is $\varepsilon$, and the price of the whole class is the parameter range divided by $\varepsilon$ — with the envelope's norm as the exchange rate.

Two currencies

The same picture, on log axes

Plot the $L_2$ size of the gap against the cell width for both kinds of class and the difference becomes a single visible fact: two straight lines of different slope. Slope one half is the mass currency; slope one is the length currency. Inverting those slopes is where $\varepsilon^2$ and $\varepsilon$ come from, and there is no third option in this course.

Why they are one thing, not two

Relabel the half-line class by $p=F(t)\in[0,1]$ instead of $t\in[0,\infty)$. Same functions, new name for the index. Now the extent is $1$, the cell of width $\Delta p$ has gap of mass $\Delta p$, and $\sqrt{\Delta p}=\varepsilon$ gives fineness $\varepsilon^2$ and count $1/\varepsilon^2$ — extent over fineness, exactly as in the length column. “Cut the probability axis” has always just meant “change to the coordinate where the range is finite”, and the quantile grid $t_j=\inf\{t:F(t)\ge j\varepsilon^2\}$ is the uniform grid on $[0,1]$ pushed back through $F^{-1}$.

Hooks worth having verbatim

Indicator, so square root, so square the budget.
Differentiate in $\theta$: if $z$ survives the derivative, $\|Z_1\|_{L_2}$ survives into the answer.
Extent over fineness, to the power of how many numbers.

Budgets

Where $\varepsilon^2/2$, $\varepsilon^2/4$ and $\varepsilon/2$ come from

When more than one parameter moves, the gap arrives as a sum of pieces — one per parameter, or one per constraint that can fail. The constants that look invented are just the arithmetic of sharing a budget between those pieces, and there are only two ways pieces combine.

Disjoint indicator pieces — split the squared budget

Their masses add before the square root, since $\|\sum_i\mathbf 1_{A_i}\|_{L_2}^2=\sum_iP(A_i)$ for disjoint $A_i$. Give each piece mass $\varepsilon^2/r$ and the total is $\varepsilon^2$, so the norm is $\varepsilon$. With $r$ pieces the grid needs $r/\varepsilon^2$ points instead of $1/\varepsilon^2$ — that is the entire difference between the $2$ in the half-line count and the $4$ in the interval count.

Pieces of any other shape — split the plain budget

Here you only have the triangle inequality, $\|\sum_ig_i\|_{L_2}\le\sum_i\|g_i\|_{L_2}$, so each piece gets norm $\varepsilon/r$. Two parameters with different units — an intercept with envelope $1$ and a slope with envelope $|z|$ — get $\varepsilon/2$ each and therefore two different finenesses, $\varepsilon/2$ and $\varepsilon/(2\|Z_1\|_{L_2})$.

Mixed gaps, in the order the arithmetic happens

Split the plain budget first, then convert. Two pieces, one indicator and one sized: each needs norm $\le\varepsilon/2$; the indicator piece therefore needs mass $\le(\varepsilon/2)^2=\varepsilon^2/4$. The $4$ is $2^2$, not a new constant, and if you ever find yourself wondering where a $4$ or an $8$ came from, it is always a squared split.

How many pieces are there? Move one parameter at a time from the low corner of the cell to the high corner. Each move is one piece, and each piece is a gap in which a single parameter moved — which you already know how to size. That telescoping is also the safest way to write it up, because it makes the cage check trivial to justify line by line.

Hook

Strips add masses. Everything else adds norms. And since only $\log \Nb$ matters, a wasteful split costs nothing at all — never spend exam minutes optimising constants.

Model answers

Three scripts, with every line explained

A · The empirical distribution function is Donsker

the template every other answer is a variation of
Put $f_t(z)=\mathbf 1\{z\le t\}$ and $\mathcal F=\{f_t:t\ge0\}$. Then $\Pn f_t=F^{(n)}(t)$ and $Pf_t=F(t)$, so $\sqrt n\big(F^{(n)}(t)-F(t)\big)=\Gn f_t$ and it suffices to prove $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Donsker.
Why first. The question is about a curve; the theorem is about a class of functions. This line is the translation, and without it nothing that follows is about the object you were asked about. Cheap and always available — write it before you know how the proof goes.
Fix $0\le t_1<\dots<t_M$. The vectors $\big(\mathbf 1\{Z_i\le t_r\}\big)_{r\le M}$ are iid and bounded, so by the multivariate CLT the vector $\big(\Gn f_{t_r}\big)_{r\le M}$ converges to a centred Gaussian with covariance $Pf_sf_t-Pf_sPf_t=F(s\wedge t)-F(s)F(t)$.
The finite-dimensional half. Bracketing gives equicontinuity only; this gives the limit. The covariance is one line because $\mathbf 1_A\mathbf 1_B=\mathbf 1_{A\cap B}$, so $Pf_sf_t=\P(Z\le s\wedge t)$. Recognise $F(s\wedge t)-F(s)F(t)$ as the covariance of $B\circ F$ with $B$ a Brownian bridge — naming the limit is worth a mark by itself.
Let $\varepsilon>0$ be given. Set $t_j=\inf\{t:F(t)\ge j\varepsilon^2\}$ for $j=0,1,2,\dots$, stopping once $j\varepsilon^2\ge1$, and $t_k=\infty$. This gives $k\le\lceil 2/\varepsilon^2\rceil$ grid points with $F(t_j-)-F(t_{j-1})\le\varepsilon^2$ for every $j$, which is possible because $F$ is non-decreasing, càdlàg and bounded by $1$.
The only real decision, and it is forced. The gap will be an indicator, so the currency is mass and the budget is $\varepsilon^2$; total mass is $1$, so $1/\varepsilon^2$ cells suffice. Two details examiners look for: the phrase let $\varepsilon>0$ be given before the grid depends on it, and the left limit $F(t_j-)$, which is what makes the argument survive atoms. Write $F(t_j)-F(t_{j-1})$ and a discrete $Z$ breaks your bound.
For $j=1,\dots,k$ define $l_j(z)=\mathbf 1_{[0,t_{j-1}]}(z)$ and $u_j(z)=\mathbf 1_{[0,t_j)}(z)$, and put $\mathcal F_j=\{f\in\mathcal F: l_j\le f\le u_j\}$. Every $f_t$ with $t\in[t_{j-1},t_j)$ lies in $\mathcal F_j$, so $\mathcal F=\bigcup_{j\le k}\mathcal F_j$.
ORDER, because $t\mapsto\mathbf 1_{[0,t]}(z)$ is non-decreasing at each fixed $z$. The cage is the family evaluated at the two ends of the cell, so it is exact and no padding is needed. The second sentence is the part people forget: you must say the brackets cover, not merely that they exist.
The cage holds pointwise: for $t\in[t_{j-1},t_j)$ and any $z$, if $z\le t_{j-1}$ then $z\le t$, and if $z\ge t_j$ then $z>t$; in either case the required inequality is an equality, and for $z$ strictly between it reads $0\le\mathbf 1_{[0,t]}(z)\le1$.
One mark, one line, almost always dropped. A bracket is a statement about every $z$ separately — that is precisely what makes it survive integration against the random empirical measure. An examiner checks whether you know this or are pattern-matching, and this sentence is how you show it.
Then $u_j-l_j=\mathbf 1_{(t_{j-1},t_j)}$, so using $\mathbf 1_A^2=\mathbf 1_A$, $\ \|u_j-l_j\|_{L_2(P)}=\big(\P(t_{j-1}<Z_1<t_j)\big)^{1/2}\le\big(F(t_j-)-F(t_{j-1})\big)^{1/2}\le\varepsilon.$
The computation the whole answer was arranged around. Three steps: identify the gap, square it (which collapses), take the root. Note the direction of the argument — the grid was chosen in the previous line so that this line ends in $\varepsilon$. In your rough work it goes the other way round: compute this first, then solve for the grid.
Hence $\Nb(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2(P))\le k\le\lceil2/\varepsilon^2\rceil$, which is polynomial in $1/\varepsilon$, so $\int_0^1\sqrt{\log \Nb(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2(P))}\,d\varepsilon<\infty$.
The invariant sentence. Identical in every question with a polynomial count. If you want to show the integral explicitly: $\log\lceil2/\varepsilon^2\rceil\le\log(2/\varepsilon^2+1)$ and $\int_{(0,1]}\sqrt{\log(2/\varepsilon^2+1)}\,d\varepsilon<\infty$ by parts, which is the separate exercise on the same sheet.
By the bracketing central limit theorem $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Donsker, so $\sqrt n(F^{(n)}-F)\rightsquigarrow B\circ F$ in $\linf([0,\infty))$, with $B$ a standard Brownian bridge. $\square$
Close in the space you were asked about. Convergence in $\linf$ is the claim; naming the limit process ties it back to the covariance you computed in line two. An answer that stops at “the entropy integral is finite” has proved half a theorem.

B · The bivariate empirical distribution function

what changes when an observation is a pair
Let $(Z_i,W_i)$ be iid with joint distribution function $H$. Put $h_{s,t}(x,y)=\mathbf 1\{x\le s\}\mathbf 1\{y\le t\}$ and $\mathcal H=\{h_{s,t}:s,t\ge0\}$, a class of functions on $[0,\infty)^2$. Then $\Pn h_{s,t}=H^{(n)}(s,t)$.
One observation is the whole pair. So every $f$ has domain $\R^2$, and $P$ is the joint law — not the margins. Everything downstream that looks new is a consequence of this one sentence, and getting it wrong makes the rest incoherent.
For arbitrary $(s_r,t_r)_{r\le M}$ the multivariate CLT gives convergence of $\big(\Gn h_{s_r,t_r}\big)_r$ to a centred Gaussian with $\operatorname{Cov}\big(\mathbb G_H(s,t),\mathbb G_H(u,v)\big)=H(s\wedge u,\,t\wedge v)-H(s,t)H(u,v)$.
Same one-line computation as the univariate case, since the product of two quadrant indicators is the indicator of the smaller quadrant: $\mathbf 1\{x\le s,y\le t\}\mathbf 1\{x\le u,y\le v\}=\mathbf 1\{x\le s\wedge u,\ y\le t\wedge v\}$. The minima appear coordinatewise.
Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose a grid $0=s_0<\dots<s_k=\infty$ on the $Z$-margin with $F_Z(s_i-)-F_Z(s_{i-1})\le\varepsilon^2/2$, and independently $0=t_0<\dots<t_k=\infty$ on the $W$-margin with the same budget; $k\le\lceil4/\varepsilon^2\rceil$ suffices for each.
Two parameters, so the squared budget splits in two — that is where $\varepsilon^2/2$ and hence $4/\varepsilon^2$ come from. The grids are built on the margins because the gap will be bounded by a union of two marginal strips, and marginal masses are all you can control.
For $s\in[s_{j-1},s_j)$ and $t\in[t_{l-1},t_l)$ set $l_{jl}(x,y)=\mathbf 1_{[0,s_{j-1}]}(x)\mathbf 1_{[0,t_{l-1}]}(y)$ and $u_{jl}(x,y)=\mathbf 1_{[0,s_j)}(x)\mathbf 1_{[0,t_l)}(y)$. Since $[0,s_{j-1}]\times[0,t_{l-1}]\subseteq[0,s]\times[0,t]\subseteq[0,s_j)\times[0,t_l)$, the cage holds at every $(x,y)$, and the $\mathcal H_{jl}$ cover $\mathcal H$.
Two indices, not one. A single index $j$ pairing the $j$-th $s$-cell with the $j$-th $t$-cell only catches matched cells, and a pair with $s$ in cell $3$ and $t$ in cell $7$ would lie in no bracket. This is the most common structural error in this question, and it is invisible unless you check the cover explicitly.
The gap is the indicator of $\big([0,s_j)\times[0,t_l)\big)\setminus\big([0,s_{j-1}]\times[0,t_{l-1}]\big)$, which is contained in $\big((s_{j-1},s_j)\times\R\big)\cup\big(\R\times(t_{l-1},t_l)\big)$. Hence $\|u_{jl}-l_{jl}\|^2_{L_2(P)}\le\P(s_{j-1}<Z_1<s_j)+\P(t_{l-1}<W_1<t_l)\le\tfrac{\varepsilon^2}2+\tfrac{\varepsilon^2}2=\varepsilon^2$.
The L-shape, bounded by two strips. A union bound is all that is available, because a joint probability is not determined by the margins. Squaring first is what lets the two masses add — this is the squared-budget split in action, and it is why each strip was given $\varepsilon^2/2$ three lines ago.
Therefore $\Nb(\varepsilon,\mathcal H,L_2(P))\le k^2\le\lceil4/\varepsilon^2\rceil^2=O(\varepsilon^{-4})$, the entropy integral converges, and $\sqrt n(H^{(n)}-H)\rightsquigarrow\mathbb G_H$ in $\linf([0,\infty)^2)$ with the covariance computed above. $\square$
The exponent counts the parameters. Two numbers, two grid choices, count squared. If your answer produces $O(\varepsilon^{-2})$ here, go back and check whether your brackets really cover the off-diagonal pairs.

C · The cumulative hazard — when there is nothing to bracket

recognise this in the first ten seconds
$\Lambda^{(n)}(t)=-\log\big(1-F^{(n)}(t)\big)$ is not of the form $\Pn f$, so there is no class to bracket. Instead write $\Lambda^{(n)}=\phi(F^{(n)})$ with $\phi(h)(t)=-\log(1-h(t))$, defined for $h$ with $h(t)<1$, and transport the known limit for $F^{(n)}$ through $\phi$.
The fork in the road. Any $\log$, ratio, inverse or supremum of an average lands here. Spending ten minutes trying to bracket $\Lambda^{(n)}$ is the single most expensive mistake available in this topic, and the diagnosis takes one line: can I write it as an average?
Domain and codomain: restrict to a compact $[0,\tau]$ with $F(\tau)<1$, take the domain to be distribution functions there and the codomain $\linf([0,\tau])$ with the uniform norm.
State it, briefly. Hadamard differentiability is a statement about maps between normed spaces, so the spaces have to be named, and $\phi$ genuinely blows up where $F=1$. One sentence is enough, and its absence is a visible gap.
For $u_n\to0$ and $h_n\to h$ uniformly, $\ \dfrac{\phi(f+u_nh_n)(t)-\phi(f)(t)}{u_n}=-\dfrac1{u_n}\log\Big(1-\dfrac{u_nh_n(t)}{1-f(t)}\Big)\longrightarrow\dfrac{h(t)}{1-f(t)},$ using $\log(1-x)\approx-x$ for small $x$. So $\phi'_f(h)=h/(1-f)$.
The algebra worth memorising. Combine the two logarithms first — $-\log(1-f-u_nh_n)+\log(1-f)=-\log\!\big(1-\tfrac{u_nh_n}{1-f}\big)$ — and the rest is the standard expansion. Convergence is uniform on $[0,\tau]$ because $1-f$ is bounded away from $0$ there, which is what the previous line bought you.
By the functional delta method applied to $\sqrt n(F^{(n)}-F)\rightsquigarrow B\circ F$, $\ \sqrt n(\Lambda^{(n)}-\Lambda)\rightsquigarrow\dfrac{B\circ F}{1-F}$ in $\linf([0,\tau])$.
The delta method needs the functional CLT as input, which is answer A. Say so explicitly — the marker wants to see that you know which result is doing the work, and that the derivative is applied to the limit process, not to the data.
The limit is centred Gaussian with $\operatorname{Cov}\Big(\dfrac{(B\circ F)(s)}{1-F(s)},\dfrac{(B\circ F)(t)}{1-F(t)}\Big)=\dfrac{F(s\wedge t)-F(s)F(t)}{(1-F(s))(1-F(t))}. \square$
Deterministic factors come straight out of the covariance, so this is one line from answer A's covariance. Divide by $(1-F(s))(1-F(t))$ and stop — there is nothing more to do, and any further manipulation risks an error for no marks.

The last line

Why you almost never have to think about the integral

The final step of every answer is the same sentence, and it is safe because the condition is extraordinarily forgiving. Move the slider and watch: any polynomial count, of any degree, with any constant, converges — the curve barely moves. The condition only bites at counts that are exponential in $1/\varepsilon$, and even then it takes a large exponent to break it.

What to say, and when the shortcut is unavailable

With a polynomial count write the invariant sentence and stop. The shortcut fails in exactly one place in this course — the class of all monotone functions, where $\log \Nb(\varepsilon)\approx\varepsilon^{-1}\log(1/\varepsilon)$ and you must evaluate the integral yourself: $\sqrt{\log \Nb(\varepsilon)}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1/2}\sqrt{\log(1/\varepsilon)}$, which is integrable near $0$ because $\int_0^1\varepsilon^{-1/2}d\varepsilon=2$. If a question is engineered so the count is not polynomial, that is the point of the question — it is testing whether you know the condition or only the shortcut.

The whole method, on one card

Class — write the statistic as an average; what is inside is $f$, what you sweep is the index.
Margins — multivariate CLT on finitely many coordinates; covariance $Pfg-Pf\,Pg$.
Cage — monotone? the two ends of the cell. Otherwise the centre, padded by $\delta m$.
Size — compute $\|u-l\|_{L_2}$ as a formula in the fineness. Indicator gives $\sqrt{\rm mass}$; anything else gives $\delta\|m\|_{L_2}$.
Count — extent over fineness, to the power of how many numbers; budget split $r$ ways if the gap has $r$ pieces.
Sentence — polynomial, so the entropy integral converges, so Donsker, so weak convergence in $\linf$.

$\Nb(\varepsilon)\le\big(\text{extent}/\text{fineness}\big)^{\#\text{params}}$

Fineness: solve $\|u-l\|_{L_2}=\varepsilon$.
Indicator gap → mass $=\varepsilon^2$, extent $1$.
Sized gap → $\delta=\varepsilon/\|m\|_{L_2}$, extent $=$ range.
$r$ strips → mass $\varepsilon^2/r$. $r$ other pieces → norm $\varepsilon/r$.
