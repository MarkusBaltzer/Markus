01 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\!\left[\left(\displaystyle\int_0^t B_s\,\mathrm dB_s\right)^{2}\right].\)
By the Itô isometry \(E\big[(\int_0^t H_s\,\mathrm dB_s)^2\big]=\int_0^t E[H_s^2]\,\mathrm ds\) with \(H_s=B_s\): \[E\!\left[\Big(\int_0^t B_s\,\mathrm dB_s\Big)^2\right]=\int_0^t E[B_s^2]\,\mathrm ds=\int_0^t s\,\mathrm ds=\frac{t^2}{2}.\]
02 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\!\left[\left(\displaystyle\int_0^1 s\,\mathrm dB_s\right)^{2}\right].\)
The integrand \(s\) is deterministic, so the integral is Gaussian and the isometry gives its variance: \[E\!\left[\Big(\int_0^1 s\,\mathrm dB_s\Big)^2\right]=\int_0^1 s^2\,\mathrm ds=\frac13.\]
03 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\!\left[\left(\displaystyle\int_0^t e^{s}\,\mathrm dB_s\right)^{2}\right].\)
By the isometry with deterministic integrand \(e^s\): \[\int_0^t (e^{s})^2\,\mathrm ds=\int_0^t e^{2s}\,\mathrm ds=\frac{e^{2t}-1}{2}.\]
04 Itô isometry
For fixed \(t\), determine \(E\!\left[\left(\displaystyle\int_0^t (t-s)\,\mathrm dB_s\right)^{2}\right].\)
Here \(t\) is a fixed parameter, so \((t-s)\) is a deterministic integrand. By the isometry \[\int_0^t (t-s)^2\,\mathrm ds=\Big[-\tfrac13(t-s)^3\Big]_0^t=\frac{t^3}{3}.\]
05 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\!\left[\left(\displaystyle\int_0^1 \mathbf 1_{\{B_s\ge 0\}}\,\mathrm dB_s\right)^{2}\right].\)
By the isometry, and since \(\mathbf 1_{\{B_s\ge0\}}^2=\mathbf 1_{\{B_s\ge0\}}\) with \(E[\mathbf 1_{\{B_s\ge0\}}]=P(B_s\ge0)=\tfrac12\): \[\int_0^1 E\big[\mathbf 1_{\{B_s\ge0\}}\big]\,\mathrm ds=\int_0^1 \tfrac12\,\mathrm ds=\frac12.\]
06 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\!\left[\left(\displaystyle\int_0^t B_s^{2}\,\mathrm dB_s\right)^{2}\right].\)
By the isometry with \(H_s=B_s^2\) and the fourth moment \(E[B_s^4]=3s^2\): \[\int_0^t E[B_s^4]\,\mathrm ds=\int_0^t 3s^2\,\mathrm ds=t^3.\]
07 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\!\left[\left(2B_t+\displaystyle\int_0^t B_s\,\mathrm dB_s\right)^{2}\right].\)
Write \(B_t=\int_0^t 1\,\mathrm dB_s\), so the whole expression is a single Itô integral: \[2B_t+\int_0^t B_s\,\mathrm dB_s=\int_0^t (2+B_s)\,\mathrm dB_s.\] By the isometry, using \(E[(2+B_s)^2]=4+E[B_s^2]=4+s\): \[\int_0^t (4+s)\,\mathrm ds=4t+\frac{t^2}{2}.\]
08 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\!\left[\left(\displaystyle\int_0^1 \mathrm dB_s\right)\left(\displaystyle\int_0^1 s\,\mathrm dB_s\right)\right].\)
The polarised isometry gives \(E\big[\int_0^T f\,\mathrm dB\,\int_0^T g\,\mathrm dB\big]=\int_0^T f\,g\,\mathrm ds\). With \(f\equiv1,\;g=s\): \[\int_0^1 1\cdot s\,\mathrm ds=\frac12.\]
09 Moments
Determine \(E[B_t^{4}]\).
\(B_t\sim\mathcal N(0,t)\). For a centred normal, \(E[X^4]=3\sigma^4\), hence \[E[B_t^4]=3t^2.\]
10 Moments
Determine \(E[B_t^{6}]\).
For \(X\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\), \(E[X^{2n}]=(2n-1)!!\,\sigma^{2n}\). Here \(n=3\): \((5)!!=5\cdot3\cdot1=15\), so \[E[B_t^6]=15\,t^3.\]
11 Moments
Determine \(\operatorname{Var}(B_t^{2})\).
\[\operatorname{Var}(B_t^2)=E[B_t^4]-\big(E[B_t^2]\big)^2=3t^2-t^2=2t^2.\]
12 Moments
For \(0\le s<t\), determine \(E[B_s^{2}B_t^{2}]\).
Split \(B_t=B_s+(B_t-B_s)\) with the increment independent of \(\mathcal F_s\): \[B_s^2B_t^2=B_s^4+2B_s^3(B_t-B_s)+B_s^2(B_t-B_s)^2.\] Taking expectations (the middle term vanishes): \[E[B_s^4]+E[B_s^2]\,E[(B_t-B_s)^2]=3s^2+s(t-s).\]
13 Moments
For \(0\le s<t\), determine \(E[B_s^{3}B_t]\).
Write \(B_t=B_s+(B_t-B_s)\): \[E[B_s^3B_t]=E[B_s^4]+E[B_s^3]\,E[B_t-B_s]=3s^2+0=3s^2.\]
14 Conditional expectation
For \(0\le s<t\), determine \(E[B_t^{2}\mid \mathcal F_s]\).
With the independent increment \(B_t-B_s\): \[E[B_t^2\mid\mathcal F_s]=E[(B_s+(B_t-B_s))^2\mid\mathcal F_s]=B_s^2+0+E[(B_t-B_s)^2]=B_s^2+(t-s).\] (Equivalently, \(B_t^2-t\) is a martingale.)
15 Conditional expectation
For \(0\le s<t\), determine \(E[B_t^{3}\mid \mathcal F_s]\).
Expand \((B_s+\Delta)^3\) with \(\Delta=B_t-B_s\sim\mathcal N(0,t-s)\), \(\Delta\perp\mathcal F_s\): \[E[B_t^3\mid\mathcal F_s]=B_s^3+3B_s^2E[\Delta]+3B_sE[\Delta^2]+E[\Delta^3]=B_s^3+3(t-s)B_s.\]
16 Conditional expectation
For \(0\le s<t\), determine \(E[e^{B_t}\mid \mathcal F_s]\).
Factor out the \(\mathcal F_s\)-measurable part and use the normal MGF on the increment: \[E[e^{B_t}\mid\mathcal F_s]=e^{B_s}\,E\big[e^{B_t-B_s}\big]=e^{B_s}e^{(t-s)/2}.\]
17 Martingales
Which of the following processes is a martingale?
By Itô, \(\mathrm d(B_t^2-t)=2B_t\,\mathrm dB_t\): pure Itô-integral term, no drift, hence a martingale. \(B_t^2\) has drift \(+\mathrm dt\); \(tB_t\) has drift \(B_t\,\mathrm dt\).
18 Martingales
Which of the following processes is a martingale?
The exponential (Doléans) martingale is \(\mathcal E_t=\exp(\theta B_t-\tfrac{\theta^2}{2}t)\). With \(\theta=1\) this is \(e^{B_t-t/2}\), and \(\mathrm d\mathcal E_t=\mathcal E_t\,\mathrm dB_t\) has no drift.
19 Martingales
For which \(\theta\) is \(e^{\theta B_t-2t}\) a martingale?
\(e^{\theta B_t-\theta^2t/2}\) is a martingale. Matching the drift term, \(\tfrac{\theta^2}{2}=2\Rightarrow\theta^2=4\Rightarrow\theta=\pm2.\)
20 Martingales
Which of the following processes is a martingale?
By Itô, \(\mathrm d(B_t^3)=3B_t^2\,\mathrm dB_t+3B_t\,\mathrm dt\) and \(\mathrm d(3tB_t)=3B_t\,\mathrm dt+3t\,\mathrm dB_t\). Hence \[\mathrm d(B_t^3-3tB_t)=(3B_t^2-3t)\,\mathrm dB_t,\] a driftless (martingale) term.
21 Martingales
Let \(X_t=B_t+t\). Which of the following is a martingale?
We need \(e^{\theta B_t-\theta^2t/2}\). Here \(e^{-2X_t}=e^{-2B_t-2t}=e^{-2B_t-\tfrac{(-2)^2}{2}t}\), which matches \(\theta=-2\). So \(e^{-2X_t}\) is a martingale.
22 Martingales
Determine \(E\big[e^{3B_t}\big]\).
Normal MGF: \(E[e^{\lambda B_t}]=e^{\lambda^2 t/2}\). With \(\lambda=3\), \(E[e^{3B_t}]=e^{9t/2}.\)
23 Martingales
Which of the following processes is not a martingale?
\(\mathrm d(B_t^2)=2B_t\,\mathrm dB_t+\mathrm dt\) has a strictly positive drift \(\mathrm dt\), so \(B_t^2\) is a submartingale, not a martingale. All others are driftless.
24 Martingales
Let \(M_t=e^{\sigma B_t-\sigma^2 t/2}\). Determine \(E[M_t^{2}]\).
\(M_t^2=e^{2\sigma B_t-\sigma^2 t}\). Write \(2\sigma B_t=(2\sigma)B_t\) and complete the martingale exponent: \[M_t^2=\underbrace{e^{2\sigma B_t-\tfrac{(2\sigma)^2}{2}t}}_{\text{mean }1}\;e^{\sigma^2 t}\quad\Rightarrow\quad E[M_t^2]=e^{\sigma^2 t}.\]
25 Gaussian processes
Determine which type of process \(e^{-t}B_{e^{2t}}\) is.
\(X_t=e^{-t}B_{e^{2t}}\) is a linear image of Brownian motion, hence Gaussian, with mean \(E[X_t]=e^{-t}E[B_{e^{2t}}]=0\). For \(s<t\), \[\operatorname{cov}(X_s,X_t)=e^{-s}e^{-t}\operatorname{cov}(B_{e^{2s}},B_{e^{2t}})=e^{-s}e^{-t}\min(e^{2s},e^{2t})=e^{-s}e^{-t}e^{2s}=e^{-(t-s)}.\] This is the stationary Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process.
26 Gaussian processes
Determine \(\operatorname{Var}\!\big(e^{-t}B_{e^{2t}}\big)\).
\[\operatorname{Var}(e^{-t}B_{e^{2t}})=e^{-2t}\operatorname{Var}(B_{e^{2t}})=e^{-2t}\cdot e^{2t}=1.\] The variance is constant — the process is (weakly) stationary.
27 Gaussian processes
Let \(X_t=B_t-tB_1\) for \(0\le t\le1\). Determine \(E[X_sX_t]\) for \(0\le s\le t\le1\).
Expand and use \(E[B_uB_v]=\min(u,v)\): \[E[X_sX_t]=E[B_sB_t]-sE[B_1B_t]-tE[B_sB_1]+stE[B_1^2]=s-st-ts+st=s(1-t).\] This is the Brownian bridge.
28 Gaussian processes
For the Brownian bridge \(X_t=B_t-tB_1\), determine \(\operatorname{Var}(X_t)\), \(0\le t\le1\).
Set \(s=t\) in \(\operatorname{cov}(X_s,X_t)=s(1-t)\), giving \(\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=t(1-t)\). It vanishes at the pinned endpoints \(t=0\) and \(t=1\).
29 Gaussian processes
For a fixed constant \(c>0\), which process is again a standard Brownian motion?
Scaling invariance: \(W_t=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt c}B_{ct}\) is Gaussian, centred, continuous, and \[\operatorname{cov}(W_s,W_t)=\tfrac1c\min(cs,ct)=\min(s,t),\] so \(W\) is a standard Brownian motion.
30 Gaussian processes
Let \(Y_t=tB_{1/t}\) for \(t>0\) (and \(Y_0=0\)). Determine \(\operatorname{cov}(Y_s,Y_t)\) for \(0<s<t\).
\[\operatorname{cov}(Y_s,Y_t)=st\,\operatorname{cov}(B_{1/s},B_{1/t})=st\,\min\!\big(\tfrac1s,\tfrac1t\big)=st\cdot\tfrac1t=s.\] So \(Y\) has the covariance \(\min(s,t)\) of a Brownian motion (time inversion).
31 Gaussian processes
For \(0<s<t\), determine the correlation \(\rho(B_s,B_t)\).
\[\rho=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(B_s,B_t)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}B_s\,\operatorname{Var}B_t}}=\frac{s}{\sqrt{s\,t}}=\sqrt{\frac{s}{t}}.\]
32 Gaussian processes
Determine the distribution of \(B_3-B_1\).
Increments are independent and \(B_t-B_s\sim\mathcal N(0,t-s)\). Here \(t-s=3-1=2\), so \(B_3-B_1\sim\mathcal N(0,2).\)
33 SDEs
Let \(\mathrm dS_t=\mu S_t\,\mathrm dt+\sigma S_t\,\mathrm dB_t\), \(S_0>0\). Determine \(E[S_t]\).
Geometric Brownian motion: \(S_t=S_0\exp\big((\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2})t+\sigma B_t\big)\). Taking the expectation with the lognormal mean, \[E[S_t]=S_0 e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t}\,E[e^{\sigma B_t}]=S_0 e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t}e^{\sigma^2 t/2}=S_0 e^{\mu t}.\]
34 SDEs
For the geometric Brownian motion \(\mathrm dS_t=\mu S_t\,\mathrm dt+\sigma S_t\,\mathrm dB_t\), determine \(\operatorname{Var}(S_t)\).
Using \(E[S_t]=S_0e^{\mu t}\) and \(E[S_t^2]=S_0^2 e^{(2\mu+\sigma^2)t}\): \[\operatorname{Var}(S_t)=S_0^2 e^{(2\mu+\sigma^2)t}-S_0^2 e^{2\mu t}=S_0^2 e^{2\mu t}\big(e^{\sigma^2 t}-1\big).\]
35 SDEs
Determine \(\mathrm d\big(e^{B_t}\big)\).
Itô's formula for \(f(x)=e^x\), \(f'=f''=e^x\): \[\mathrm d(e^{B_t})=e^{B_t}\,\mathrm dB_t+\tfrac12 e^{B_t}\,\mathrm dt.\] The \(\tfrac12\,\mathrm dt\) term is the Itô correction absent in ordinary calculus.
36 SDEs
Solve \(\mathrm dX_t=X_t\,\mathrm dB_t,\; X_0=1.\)
This is the SDE of the exponential martingale. Its solution is the Doléans exponential \[X_t=\exp\big(B_t-\tfrac12 t\big),\] which one checks by Itô's formula gives back \(\mathrm dX_t=X_t\,\mathrm dB_t\).
37 SDEs
Determine \(\mathrm d\big(B_t^{3}\big)\).
Itô with \(f(x)=x^3\), \(f'=3x^2\), \(f''=6x\): \[\mathrm d(B_t^3)=3B_t^2\,\mathrm dB_t+\tfrac12\cdot6B_t\,\mathrm dt=3B_t^2\,\mathrm dB_t+3B_t\,\mathrm dt.\]
38 SDEs
For the Vasicek model \(\mathrm dr_t=a(b-r_t)\,\mathrm dt+\sigma\,\mathrm dB_t\), determine \(E[r_t]\).
Let \(m(t)=E[r_t]\). Taking expectations removes the martingale term: \(m'(t)=a(b-m(t))\). Solving the linear ODE with \(m(0)=r_0\): \[m(t)=b+(r_0-b)e^{-at}\;\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}\;b.\]
39 SDEs
Using integration by parts, express \(\displaystyle\int_0^t s\,\mathrm dB_s\).
Since \(s\) has finite variation, \(\mathrm d(sB_s)=B_s\,\mathrm ds+s\,\mathrm dB_s\) (no Itô correction). Integrating from \(0\) to \(t\): \[tB_t=\int_0^t B_s\,\mathrm ds+\int_0^t s\,\mathrm dB_s\quad\Rightarrow\quad\int_0^t s\,\mathrm dB_s=tB_t-\int_0^t B_s\,\mathrm ds.\]
40 SDEs
For the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process \(\mathrm dX_t=-\theta X_t\,\mathrm dt+\sigma\,\mathrm dB_t\), \(X_0=0\), determine \(\operatorname{Var}(X_t)\).
The solution is \(X_t=\sigma\int_0^t e^{-\theta(t-u)}\,\mathrm dB_u\). By the Itô isometry, \[\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=\sigma^2\int_0^t e^{-2\theta(t-u)}\,\mathrm du=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\big(1-e^{-2\theta t}\big)\;\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}\;\frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}.\]
41 Quadratic variation
Determine the quadratic variation \(\langle X,X\rangle_t\) of \(X_t=\displaystyle\int_0^t B_s\,\mathrm dB_s\).
For \(\mathrm dX_t=H_t\,\mathrm dB_t\) the quadratic variation is \(\mathrm d\langle X\rangle_t=H_t^2\,\mathrm d\langle B\rangle_t=H_t^2\,\mathrm dt\). With \(H_t=B_t\): \[\langle X,X\rangle_t=\int_0^t B_s^2\,\mathrm ds.\]
42 Quadratic variation
Determine \(\langle X,X\rangle_t\) for \(X_t=\displaystyle\int_0^t s\,\mathrm dB_s\).
\(\mathrm d\langle X\rangle_t=s^2\,\mathrm dt\), so \[\langle X,X\rangle_t=\int_0^t s^2\,\mathrm ds=\frac{t^3}{3}.\] (Note it equals \(E[X_t^2]\) here since the integrand is deterministic.)
43 Quadratic variation
Determine \(\langle X,X\rangle_t\) for \(X_t=\sigma B_t+\mu t\).
The finite-variation drift \(\mu t\) contributes nothing to quadratic variation: \[\langle X,X\rangle_t=\sigma^2\langle B,B\rangle_t=\sigma^2 t.\]
44 Quadratic variation
Determine the covariation \(\big\langle B,\;\textstyle\int_0^{\cdot}B_s\,\mathrm dB_s\big\rangle_t\).
With \(\mathrm dX_t=B_t\,\mathrm dB_t\), the covariation is \(\mathrm d\langle B,X\rangle_t=B_t\,\mathrm d\langle B\rangle_t=B_t\,\mathrm dt\), hence \[\big\langle B,X\big\rangle_t=\int_0^t B_s\,\mathrm ds.\]
45 Quadratic variation
For the geometric Brownian motion \(\mathrm dS_t=\mu S_t\,\mathrm dt+\sigma S_t\,\mathrm dB_t\), determine \(\langle S,S\rangle_t\).
Only the diffusion coefficient \(\sigma S_t\) enters: \[\mathrm d\langle S\rangle_t=(\sigma S_t)^2\,\mathrm dt\quad\Rightarrow\quad\langle S,S\rangle_t=\sigma^2\int_0^t S_s^2\,\mathrm ds.\]
46 Stopping times
By the reflection principle, determine \(P\!\left(\max_{0\le s\le t}B_s\ge a\right)\) for \(a>0\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF.
The reflection principle gives \(P(\max_{s\le t}B_s\ge a)=2P(B_t\ge a)\). Since \(B_t/\sqrt t\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\), \[2P(B_t\ge a)=2\big(1-\Phi(a/\sqrt t)\big).\]
47 Stopping times
Let \(\tau_a=\inf\{t\ge0: B_t=a\}\) for \(a>0\). Determine \(E[\tau_a]\).
Brownian motion is recurrent, so \(P(\tau_a<\infty)=1\); however the hitting time is heavy-tailed (\(P(\tau_a>t)\sim c/\sqrt t\)) and \[E[\tau_a]=\infty.\] (If \(E[\tau_a]\) were finite, optional stopping on the martingale \(B_t^2-t\) would give \(a^2=E[\tau_a]\), but the required uniform integrability fails.)
48 Stopping times
A Brownian motion starts at \(0\) and \(\tau\) is the first exit time from \((-a,b)\), \(a,b>0\). Determine \(P(B_\tau=b)\), i.e. the probability of hitting \(b\) before \(-a\).
\(B_{t\wedge\tau}\) is a bounded martingale, so \(E[B_\tau]=0\). With \(p=P(B_\tau=b)\), \[b\,p+(-a)(1-p)=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad p=\frac{a}{a+b}.\]
49 Stopping times
Let \(\tau\) be the first exit time of \(B\) (started at \(0\)) from \((-a,a)\), \(a>0\). Determine \(E[\tau]\).
\(B_t^2-t\) is a martingale, so \(E[B_\tau^2]=E[\tau]\). By symmetry \(B_\tau=\pm a\), hence \(B_\tau^2=a^2\) and \[E[\tau]=E[B_\tau^2]=a^2.\]
50 Stopping times
Which of the following random variables is a stopping time (with respect to the Brownian filtration)?
Each candidate is \(\mathcal F_2\)-measurable and takes two values \(v_1\) (on \(\{B_2>1\}\)) and \(v_2\) (on \(\{B_2\le1\}\)). Such a variable is a stopping time iff both values are \(\ge2\), so that \(\{T\le t\}\) is empty for \(t<2\) and lies in \(\mathcal F_2\subseteq\mathcal F_t\) for \(t\ge2\). Only \(2\,\mathbf 1_{\{B_2>1\}}+4\,\mathbf 1_{\{B_2\le1\}}\) has both values \(\ge2\).
Notation: \(B=(B_t)_{t\ge0}\) is a standard Brownian motion and \(\mathcal F=(\mathcal F_t)_{t\ge0}\) its completed canonical filtration. \(\Phi\) denotes the standard normal CDF. Single correct answer per question.
