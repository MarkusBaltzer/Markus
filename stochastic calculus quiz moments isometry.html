01 Parity
Determine \(E\big[B_s^3B_t^2\big]\) for \(0\le s
The monomial has total degree \(3+2=5\), which is odd. The vector \((B_s,B_t)\) is centred Gaussian, so \((B_s,B_t)\overset{d}{=}(-B_s,-B_t)\); an odd-degree monomial changes sign under this symmetry, hence equals minus itself in expectation. Always check the total degree first — it settles half the exam questions of this type in one line, with no computation at all.
02 Parity
Which of the following expectations vanishes by parity alone (for \(0
Only \(B_s^2B_t^3\) has odd total degree (\(5\)). The others have even degree and are generally non-zero: \(E[B_s^2B_t^2]=st+2s^2\), \(E[B_s^3B_t]=3s^2\), \(E[B_sB_t]=s\), \(E[B_s^4]=3s^2\). Parity is about the sum of the exponents, not about individual factors.
03 Parity
Why is \(E\big[B_{t_1}^{k_1}\cdots B_{t_n}^{k_n}\big]=0\) whenever \(k_1+\dots+k_n\) is odd?
Symmetry of the joint law is the whole argument: \(X\overset{d}{=}-X\) for a centred Gaussian vector, so any odd function of it has expectation \(-\) itself. \(E[B_t]=0\) alone is far too weak (it would not give \(E[B_s^2B_t^3]=0\)), and independence of increments is not needed — the statement holds for any centred Gaussian process, e.g. the Brownian bridge.
04 Parity
Determine \(E[B_sB_tB_u]\) for \(0\le s\le t\le u\).
Total degree \(3\): odd, so the expectation is \(0\) whatever the times are. A frequent slip is to reach for the tower property here: \(E[B_sB_tB_u]=E[B_sB_tE[B_u\mid\mathcal F_t]]=E[B_sB_t^2]\), which is again odd and zero. Parity gets there immediately.
05 Parity
Determine \(E\big[B_1^2B_2B_3\big]\).
Total degree \(4\) is even, so parity gives nothing and one must compute. Wick/Isserlis with \(X=B_1\) (twice), \(Y=B_2\), \(Z=B_3\) sums over the three pairings: \[E[X^2YZ]=E[X^2]E[YZ]+2E[XY]E[XZ]=1\cdot2+2\cdot1\cdot1=4.\] Equivalently by conditioning: \(E[B_1^2B_2B_3]=E[B_1^2B_2^2]=1\cdot2+2\cdot1^2=4\).
06 Parity

Conditioning gives \(E[\operatorname{sgn}(B_s)B_t]=E[\operatorname{sgn}(B_s)B_s]=E|B_s|=\sqrt{2s/\pi}>0\). 07 Parity
Determine \(E\big[B_s^5B_t\big]\) for \(0\le s
Degree \(6\) is even, so compute. Split \(B_t=B_s+(B_t-B_s)\) with the increment independent and centred: \[E[B_s^5B_t]=E[B_s^6]+E[B_s^5]E[B_t-B_s]=15s^3.\] Note the answer does not involve \(t\) at all: conditioning on \(\mathcal F_s\) replaces \(B_t\) by \(B_s\) whenever \(B_t\) appears to the first power.
08 Parity
Determine \(E\big[B_s^2(B_t-B_s)^3\big]\) for \(0\le s
Two independent factors, so the expectation splits: \(E[B_s^2]\cdot E[(B_t-B_s)^3]=s\cdot0=0\), the third moment of a centred Gaussian being zero. This is the workhorse move for mixed moments: rewrite everything in terms of \(B_s\) and the independent increment, then any factor with an odd power of the increment drops out.
09 Gaussian moments
Determine \(E[B_t^6]\).
\(B_t=\sqrt t\,Z\) with \(Z\sim N(0,1)\), and \(E[Z^{2k}]=(2k-1)!!\). For \(2k=6\): \(5!!=5\cdot3\cdot1=15\), so \(E[B_t^6]=15t^3\). Keep the short list to hand: \(E[Z^2]=1\), \(E[Z^4]=3\), \(E[Z^6]=15\), \(E[Z^8]=105\), and the power of \(t\) is always half the power of \(B\).
10 Gaussian moments
For \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) and \(k\ge1\), determine \(E[Z^{2k}]\).
The double factorial counts the number of ways to split \(2k\) points into pairs, which is exactly the number of terms in the Wick/Isserlis expansion (each pairing contributes \(E[Z^2]^k=1\)). For \(2k=4\) this gives \(3\) pairings, for \(2k=6\) it gives \(15\). Note \(2^kk!\) is the wrong classic: that is \(E[Z^{2k}]\)'s cousin \((2k)!!\).
11 Gaussian moments
Determine \(\operatorname{var}(B_t^2)\).
\(\operatorname{var}(B_t^2)=E[B_t^4]-\big(E[B_t^2]\big)^2=3t^2-t^2=2t^2\). This single number is behind several standard results: the \(L^2\)-convergence of the quadratic variation sums, the identity \(E[(\int_0^tB\,\mathrm dB)^2]=\tfrac12t^2\), and the fact that \(B_t^2-t\) has variance \(2t^2\).

13 Gaussian moments
Determine \(\operatorname{cov}(B_s^2,B_t^2)\) for \(0\le s
\(\operatorname{cov}(B_s^2,B_t^2)=E[B_s^2B_t^2]-st=(st+2s^2)-st=2s^2\). Striking feature: the answer does not depend on \(t\) at all. Freezing the earlier time freezes the covariance, however far into the future you look — the extra randomness after \(s\) is independent and contributes only to the variance of \(B_t^2\).
14 Gaussian moments
Determine \(\operatorname{corr}(B_s^2,B_t^2)\) for \(0
\(\dfrac{2s^2}{\sqrt{2s^2\cdot2t^2}}=\dfrac{2s^2}{2st}=\dfrac st\). Compare \(\operatorname{corr}(B_s,B_t)=s/\sqrt{st}=\sqrt{s/t}\): squaring the process squares the correlation. Both tend to \(0\) as \(t\to\infty\), but the squared process decorrelates faster.
15 Gaussian moments
Determine \(E\big[B_1^2B_2^4\big]\).
Split off the independent increment \(D=B_2-B_1\sim N(0,1)\): \[E[B_1^2(B_1+D)^4]=E[B_1^6]+6E[B_1^4]E[D^2]+E[B_1^2]E[D^4]=15+18+3=36,\] the odd-power terms vanishing. In general \(E[B_s^2B_t^4]=3st^2+12s^2t\); with \(s=1,t=2\) this is \(12+24=36\).
16 Gaussian moments
Determine \(E\big[B_2^4B_3^2\big]\).
\(E[B_s^4B_t^2]=E[B_s^4(B_s+D)^2]=E[B_s^6]+E[B_s^4]E[D^2]=15s^3+3s^2(t-s)=12s^3+3s^2t\). With \(s=2,t=3\): \(12\cdot8+3\cdot4\cdot3=96+36=132\). Note how differently the two exponents enter — \(E[B_s^4B_t^2]\neq E[B_s^2B_t^4]\), since the earlier time carries all the shared randomness.
17 Gaussian moments
Determine \(E\big[B_s^3B_t^3\big]\) for \(0\le s
\(E[B_s^3(B_s+D)^3]=E[B_s^6]+3E[B_s^4]E[D^2]=15s^3+9s^2(t-s)=6s^3+9s^2t\). Wick gives the same split: \(6\) pairings match every \(B_s\) with a \(B_t\) (\(6E[B_sB_t]^3=6s^3\)) and \(9\) pairings use one \(B_s\)–\(B_s\) pair (\(9E[B_s^2]E[B_sB_t]E[B_t^2]=9s^2t\)). Sanity check at \(s=t\): \(6t^3+9t^3=15t^3=E[B_t^6]\).
18 Gaussian moments
Determine \(E\big[B_2^3B_5^3\big]\).
Using \(E[B_s^3B_t^3]=6s^3+9s^2t\) with \(s=2,t=5\): \(6\cdot8+9\cdot4\cdot5=48+180=228\). A quick plausibility check: it must exceed \(E[B_2^6]=15\cdot8=120\) and stay below \(\sqrt{E[B_2^6]E[B_5^6]}=\sqrt{120\cdot1875}\approx474\) by Cauchy–Schwarz.
19 Gaussian moments
How many terms does the Wick/Isserlis expansion of \(E[X_1X_2X_3X_4X_5X_6]\) have, for a centred Gaussian vector?
The expectation is the sum over all perfect matchings of \(\{1,\dots,6\}\), and there are \(5!!=5\cdot3\cdot1=15\) of them (\(3\) for four variables, \(15\) for six, \(105\) for eight). Consistency check: with all \(X_i=Z\sim N(0,1)\) every term equals \(1\), giving \(E[Z^6]=15\).
20 Gaussian moments
Determine \(E\big[B_1B_2B_3B_4\big]\).
Three pairings, each a product of two covariances \(\min(\cdot,\cdot)\): \[E[B_1B_2]E[B_3B_4]+E[B_1B_3]E[B_2B_4]+E[B_1B_4]E[B_2B_3]=1\cdot3+1\cdot2+1\cdot2=7.\] Degree \(4\) is even, so parity gives nothing; note that every pairing containing \(B_1\) contributes a factor \(1\), which is why the earliest time dominates.
21 Increment splitting
Determine \(E\big[B_t^2\mid\mathcal F_s\big]\) for \(s
Write \(B_t=B_s+D\) with \(D\sim N(0,t-s)\) independent of \(\mathcal F_s\): \(E[(B_s+D)^2\mid\mathcal F_s]=B_s^2+2B_sE[D]+E[D^2]=B_s^2+(t-s)\). Equivalently, this is the martingale property of \(B_t^2-t\).
22 Increment splitting
Determine \(E\big[B_t^3\mid\mathcal F_s\big]\) for \(s
Expanding \((B_s+D)^3\) and keeping the terms with even powers of \(D\): \(B_s^3+3B_sE[D^2]=B_s^3+3(t-s)B_s\). Rearranged, \(B_t^3-3tB_t\) is a martingale — the cubic Hermite polynomial.
23 Increment splitting
Determine \(E\big[B_t^4\mid\mathcal F_s\big]\) for \(s
\((B_s+D)^4=B_s^4+4B_s^3D+6B_s^2D^2+4B_sD^3+D^4\); the odd powers of \(D\) drop, leaving \(B_s^4+6(t-s)B_s^2+3(t-s)^2\) with \(E[D^4]=3(t-s)^2\). Setting \(s=0\) recovers \(E[B_t^4]=3t^2\), a useful check on the coefficients.
24 Increment splitting
Determine \(E\big[B_s^2B_t^2\big]\) for \(0\le s
\(E[B_s^2B_t^2]=E\big[B_s^2E[B_t^2\mid\mathcal F_s]\big]=E\big[B_s^2(B_s^2+t-s)\big]=3s^2+s(t-s)=st+2s^2\). This is the practice-quiz answer \(s(t-s)+3s^2\) in a different arrangement. Two routes, one number: Wick pairings and the tower property should always agree, and checking them against each other is the cheapest way to catch an arithmetic slip under time pressure.
25 Increment splitting
Determine \(E\big[B_sB_t^3\big]\) for \(0\le s
Tower: \(E[B_sE[B_t^3\mid\mathcal F_s]]=E[B_s(B_s^3+3(t-s)B_s)]=3s^2+3(t-s)s=3st\). Wick agrees: the three pairings each contain one \(B_sB_t\) factor and one \(B_t^2\) factor, giving \(3E[B_sB_t]E[B_t^2]=3st\). Compare \(E[B_s^3B_t]=3s^2\): swapping which time carries the cube changes the answer completely.
26 Increment splitting
Determine \(E\big[B_s^2(B_t-B_s)^2\big]\) for \(0\le s
The two factors are independent, so the expectation factorises: \(E[B_s^2]E[(B_t-B_s)^2]=s(t-s)\). This is the term that survives in the practice-quiz computation \(E[B_s^2B_t^2]=E[B_s^2(B_t-B_s)^2]+E[B_s^4]+2E[B_s^3]E[B_t-B_s]=s(t-s)+3s^2\).
27 Increment splitting
Determine \(E\big[(B_t-B_s)^4\big]\) for \(0\le s
Increments are stationary: \(B_t-B_s\overset{d}{=}B_{t-s}\sim N(0,t-s)\), so the fourth moment is \(3(t-s)^2\). The distractor \(3t^2-3s^2\) comes from differencing \(E[B_t^4]\) and \(E[B_s^4]\), which is meaningless — moments do not subtract like that.
28 Increment splitting
Determine \(E\big[B_sB_t\mid\mathcal F_r\big]\) for \(0\le r\le s\le t\).
Condition in stages, innermost time last: \(E[B_sB_t\mid\mathcal F_r]=E\big[B_sE[B_t\mid\mathcal F_s]\mid\mathcal F_r\big]=E[B_s^2\mid\mathcal F_r]=B_r^2+(s-r)\). The later time \(t\) disappears entirely, because \(B_t\) enters linearly. Taking \(r=0\) gives \(E[B_sB_t]=s\).
29 Increment splitting
Determine \(E\big[B_s^4B_t^2\big]\) for \(0\le s
\(E[B_s^4(B_s+D)^2]=E[B_s^6]+E[B_s^4]E[D^2]=15s^3+3s^2(t-s)=12s^3+3s^2t\). Check at \(s=t\): \(12t^3+3t^3=15t^3=E[B_t^6]\). Any formula for a mixed moment should collapse to the pure moment when the two times coincide — a free correctness test.
30 Increment splitting
Determine \(E\big[B_1^2B_2^2B_3^2\big]\).
Peel off the last increment \(D_2=B_3-B_2\sim N(0,1)\), independent of \(\mathcal F_2\): \[E[B_1^2B_2^2B_3^2]=E[B_1^2B_2^2(B_2+D_2)^2]=E[B_1^2B_2^4]+E[B_1^2B_2^2]\cdot1=36+4=40,\] using \(E[B_1^2B_2^4]=36\) and \(E[B_1^2B_2^2]=1\cdot2+2\cdot1=4\). Work from the latest time inwards: each step reduces the number of distinct times by one.
31 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\Big[\big(\int_0^tB_s^2\,\mathrm dB_s\big)^2\Big]\).
Isometry then a Gaussian moment: \(\int_0^tE[B_s^4]\,\mathrm ds=\int_0^t3s^2\,\mathrm ds=t^3\). The order matters — square the integrand first, then take expectations, then integrate in \(s\). Squaring \(E[B_s^2]=s\) instead would give the wrong \(\tfrac13t^3\).
32 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\Big[\big(\int_0^tB_s^3\,\mathrm dB_s\big)^2\Big]\).
\(\int_0^tE[B_s^6]\,\mathrm ds=\int_0^t15s^3\,\mathrm ds=\tfrac{15}4t^4\). Pattern worth memorising: \(E[(\int_0^tB_s^n\,\mathrm dB_s)^2]=\int_0^t(2n-1)!!\,s^n\,\mathrm ds =\frac{(2n-1)!!}{n+1}t^{n+1}\).
33 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\Big[\big(B_t+\int_0^ts\,\mathrm dB_s\big)^2\Big]\).
Write \(B_t=\int_0^t1\,\mathrm dB_s\) and merge the integrands, exactly as in the practice quiz: \[E\Big[\Big(\int_0^t(1+s)\,\mathrm dB_s\Big)^2\Big]=\int_0^t(1+s)^2\,\mathrm ds=\frac{(1+t)^3-1}{3}=t+t^2+\tfrac13t^3.\] Since the integrand is deterministic no expectation is needed inside; the cross term \(2\int_0^ts\,\mathrm ds=t^2\) is exactly what is lost if you (wrongly) treat the two pieces as uncorrelated.
34 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\Big[\big(2B_t-\int_0^tB_s\,\mathrm dB_s\big)^2\Big]\).
Merge into one integral: \(\int_0^t(2-B_s)\,\mathrm dB_s\), so by the isometry \[\int_0^tE\big[(2-B_s)^2\big]\,\mathrm ds=\int_0^t\big(4-4E[B_s]+E[B_s^2]\big)\,\mathrm ds=\int_0^t(4+s)\,\mathrm ds =4t+\tfrac12t^2.\] The cross term vanishes because \(E[B_s]=0\), not because the two integrals are independent — they are not.
35 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\Big[\big(\int_0^tB_s\,\mathrm dB_s\big)\big(\int_0^ts\,\mathrm dB_s\big)\Big]\).
Polarised isometry: \(E[\int f\,\mathrm dB\int g\,\mathrm dB]=\int_0^tE[f_sg_s]\,\mathrm ds =\int_0^tsE[B_s]\,\mathrm ds=0\). So the two integrals are uncorrelated — but not independent: the second is Gaussian, the first is \(\tfrac12(B_t^2-t)\), and both are functions of the same path.
36 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\Big[B_t\int_0^ts\,\mathrm dB_s\Big]\).
Again write \(B_t=\int_0^t1\,\mathrm dB_s\) and polarise: \(\int_0^t1\cdot s\,\mathrm ds=\tfrac12t^2\). Contrast with the previous question, where the random integrand \(B_s\) had mean zero and killed the cross term. Turning \(B_t\) into a stochastic integral with integrand \(1\) is the trick that makes both computations one line.
37 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\Big[\big(\int_0^t|B_s|\,\mathrm dB_s\big)^2\Big]\).
\(\int_0^tE\big[|B_s|^2\big]\,\mathrm ds=\int_0^ts\,\mathrm ds=\tfrac12t^2\): the absolute value disappears on squaring, so this matches \(E[(\int_0^tB_s\,\mathrm dB_s)^2]\). The trap is to use \(E|B_s|=\sqrt{2s/\pi}\) and square it, giving \(\tfrac2\pi\cdot\tfrac12t^2\). The isometry wants the second moment of the integrand, never the square of its mean.
38 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\Big[\big(\int_0^t\mathbf 1_{\{B_s>0\}}B_s\,\mathrm dB_s\big)^2\Big]\).
\(\int_0^tE\big[B_s^2\mathbf 1_{\{B_s>0\}}\big]\,\mathrm ds=\int_0^t\tfrac s2\,\mathrm ds=\tfrac14t^2\), using symmetry: \(B_s^2\mathbf 1_{\{B_s>0\}}\) and \(B_s^2\mathbf 1_{\{B_s<0\}}\) have the same expectation and add up to \(E[B_s^2]=s\). Exactly half of the previous answer — the indicator halves the squared integrand in mean, and the isometry is linear in that quantity.
39 Itô isometry
Let \(X_t=\int_0^ts\,\mathrm dB_s\). Determine \(E\Big[\big(\int_0^tX_s\,\mathrm dB_s\big)^2\Big]\).
Two applications of the isometry, exactly as in question 7 of the practice quiz. Inner: \(E[X_s^2]=\int_0^su^2\,\mathrm du=\tfrac13s^3\). Outer: \(\int_0^tE[X_s^2]\,\mathrm ds=\int_0^t\tfrac13s^3\,\mathrm ds=\tfrac1{12}t^4\).
40 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\Big[\big(\int_0^t(B_s+s)\,\mathrm dB_s\big)^2\Big]\).
\(E[(B_s+s)^2]=E[B_s^2]+2sE[B_s]+s^2=s+s^2\), so the isometry gives \(\int_0^t(s+s^2)\,\mathrm ds=\tfrac12t^2+\tfrac13t^3\). The mixed term \(2sE[B_s]\) vanishing is what keeps the answer clean; note it is an expectation of a product inside the time integral, not a product of expectations of the two integrals.
41 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\Big[\big(\int_0^te^{B_s}\,\mathrm dB_s\big)^2\Big]\).
\(\int_0^tE[e^{2B_s}]\,\mathrm ds=\int_0^te^{2s}\,\mathrm ds=\tfrac12(e^{2t}-1)\), using \(E[e^{\theta B_s}]=e^{\theta^2s/2}\) with \(\theta=2\). Squaring the integrand doubles \(\theta\), so the exponent picks up a factor \(4\) before halving — using \(\theta=1\) by mistake gives the distractor \(e^t-1\).
42 Itô isometry
Determine \(E\Big[\big(3B_t+\int_0^tB_s\,\mathrm dB_s\big)^2\Big]\).
One integral with integrand \(3+B_s\): \(\int_0^tE[(3+B_s)^2]\,\mathrm ds=\int_0^t(9+s)\,\mathrm ds=9t+\tfrac12t^2\). The practice-quiz question is the case with coefficient \(1\), giving \(t+\tfrac12t^2\); the coefficient enters squared, and the cross term dies because \(E[B_s]=0\).
43 Scaling & laws
Which identity is the basis for computing \(E[B_s^{2k}]\) from standard normal moments?
Brownian scaling in its simplest form: \(B_s\) is centred Gaussian with variance \(s\), so \(B_s\overset{d}{=}\sqrt s\,Z\) and \(E[B_s^{2k}]=s^kE[Z^{2k}]=s^k(2k-1)!!\). The exponent bookkeeping (\(s^k\), not \(s^{2k}\)) is where most sign-free errors happen; note the practice quiz uses precisely this to write \(B_s=\sqrt s\,B_1\) in the \(E[B_s^2B_t^2]\) computation.
44 Scaling & laws
Determine \(E\big[B_t^+\big]=E\big[\max(B_t,0)\big]\).
By symmetry \(E[B_t^+]=\tfrac12E|B_t|=\tfrac12\sqrt{2t/\pi}=\sqrt{t/(2\pi)}\). Useful as the \(K=0\) case of a call price, and a reminder that \(E[B_t]=0\) splits into two equal and opposite halves \(E[B_t^+]=E[B_t^-]\).
45 Scaling & laws
Determine \(E\big[(B_t^+)^2\big]\).
\((B_t^+)^2=B_t^2\mathbf 1_{\{B_t>0\}}\), and by symmetry this carries exactly half of \(E[B_t^2]=t\). Same argument as the indicator isometry question; and note \(\operatorname{var}(B_t^+)=t/2-t/(2\pi)\), so the positive part is not Gaussian even though \(B_t\) is.
46 Scaling & laws
Determine \(\operatorname{var}\big(|B_t|\big)\).
\(E[|B_t|^2]=t\) and \(E|B_t|=\sqrt{2t/\pi}\), so \(\operatorname{var}(|B_t|)=t-\tfrac2\pi t=(1-\tfrac2\pi)t\approx0.363\,t\). Folding the distribution at \(0\) keeps the second moment and creates a positive mean, hence strictly reduces the variance.
47 Scaling & laws
Determine \(E\big[e^{2B_3}\big]\).
\(E[e^{\theta B_t}]=e^{\theta^2t/2}\); with \(\theta=2\) and \(t=3\) this is \(e^{4\cdot3/2}=e^6\). This is the moment generating function of \(N(0,t)\), and it is what makes \(e^{\theta B_t-\theta^2t/2}\) a martingale.
48 Scaling & laws
Determine \(\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}E\big[\mathbf 1_{\{B_t\le5\}}\big]\).
\(E[\mathbf 1_{\{B_t\le5\}}]=P(\sqrt t\,B_1\le5)=P(B_1\le5/\sqrt t)\to P(B_1\le0)=\tfrac12\). The fixed level becomes negligible against the spreading \(\sqrt t\) scale, so any fixed threshold gives \(\tfrac12\) in the limit. (For \(t\to0\) the same expression tends to \(1\).)
49 Scaling & laws
Let \(Y_t=B_t-\tfrac t2B_2\) for \(0\le t\le2\). Determine \(E[Y_sY_t]\) for \(0\le s\le t\le2\).
Expand and use \(E[B_uB_v]=\min(u,v)\): \[E[Y_sY_t]=s-\tfrac t2s-\tfrac s2t+\tfrac{st}4\cdot2=s-\tfrac{st}2=s\Big(1-\frac t2\Big).\] This is the bridge on \([0,2]\); the practice-quiz case \(X_t=B_t-tB_1\) with covariance \(s(1-t)\) is the horizon-\(1\) version. Check the endpoint: \(t=2\) gives \(0\), as it must since \(Y_2=0\).
50 Scaling & laws
Determine \(E\big[|B_t|^3\big]\).
Scaling first: \(E|B_t|^3=t^{3/2}E|Z|^3\). For a standard normal, \(E|Z|^3=2\int_0^\infty z^3\varphi(z)\,\mathrm dz=2\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx1.596\). Note that odd absolute moments are not zero and are not given by the double-factorial rule — that rule applies to even powers of \(Z\) itself.
Notation: \(B=(B_t)_{t\ge0}\) is a standard Brownian motion, \(\mathcal F=(\mathcal F_t)_{t\ge0}\) its completed canonical filtration, \(Z\sim N(0,1)\), and \((2k-1)!!=(2k-1)(2k-3)\cdots3\cdot1\). Single correct answer per question.
