How the bracketing proof actually works

Nothing here is quoted before it is derived. We start with a single average, find out exactly what goes wrong when you want to control many averages at once, and let the failure tell us what a bracket has to be. By the end of §6 you will have read one complete proof with no gaps, by the end of §8 you will know precisely which step is hard and why, and §10 runs the finished machine on twelve different classes with every line of algebra written out.

The promise

If at any point something appears without a reason, that is a bug in this page. Two things are stated without proof, and both are flagged where they occur: Bernstein's inequality (§3) and the final assembly of the chaining argument (§8). Everything else — including the whole of §6 — is derived here in full.

§1Everything in sight is an average

We have $Z_1,\dots,Z_n$ iid with distribution $P$. Take any function $f$ of one observation. Then

$$\mathbb P^{(n)}f\;:=\;\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nf(Z_i)$$

is just a sample average, and $Pf:=\mathbb E[f(Z_1)]$ is the number it estimates. Two facts from a first probability course, and we will use nothing deeper about a single $f$:

$$\mathbb E\big[\mathbb P^{(n)}f\big]=Pf,\qquad \operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb P^{(n)}f\big)=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(f(Z_1))}{n}.$$

The error is therefore of size $n^{-1/2}$, so to see it we rescale. Define the empirical process applied to $f$:

$$\mathbb G^{(n)}f\;:=\;\sqrt n\big(\mathbb P^{(n)}f-Pf\big)=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\Big(f(Z_i)-Pf\Big).$$

This is a centred, normalised sum of iid terms. Its mean is $0$; its variance is $\operatorname{Var}(f(Z_1))$, independent of $n$; and by the CLT it converges to $\mathcal N\big(0,\operatorname{Var}(f(Z_1))\big)$. Nothing new so far.

The one piece of translation you need

The distribution function is this construction with a particular $f$. Put $f_t(z)=\mathbf 1_{[0,t]}(z)$, which is $1$ if $z\le t$ and $0$ otherwise. Then $$\mathbb P^{(n)}f_t=\frac1n\#\{i:Z_i\le t\}=F^{(n)}(t),\qquad Pf_t=\mathbb P(Z_1\le t)=F(t),$$ $$\mathbb G^{(n)}f_t=\sqrt n\big(F^{(n)}(t)-F(t)\big).$$ So “the rescaled error of the empirical distribution function at $t$” and “the empirical process applied to the function $f_t$” are the same object. Studying a family of functions $\mathcal F=\{f_t:t\ge0\}$ is the same as studying the whole curve.

One more elementary fact, used constantly below, so let us record it now. For an indicator, squaring changes nothing: $\mathbf 1_A^2=\mathbf 1_A$. Hence

$$\mathbb E\big[\mathbf 1_A(Z_1)^2\big]=\mathbb E\big[\mathbf 1_A(Z_1)\big]=P(A), \qquad\text{so}\qquad \|\mathbf 1_A\|_{L_2(P)}=\sqrt{P(A)} .$$

Here $\|g\|_{L_2(P)}=\big(\mathbb E[g(Z_1)^2]\big)^{1/2}$ and $\|g\|_{L_1(P)}=\mathbb E|g(Z_1)|$. Remember the box: for a set, the $L_2$ size is the square root of the probability, while the $L_1$ size is the probability itself. This single asymmetry is responsible for every $\varepsilon^2$ you will meet.

§2One function, $N$ functions, all functions

Fix the goal: we want to say something about

$$\Big\|\mathbb G^{(n)}\Big\|_{\mathcal F}:=\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\big|\mathbb G^{(n)}f\big| ,$$

because that is what a statement “uniformly in $t$” means. Watch what happens as the family grows.

One function

$\mathbb G^{(n)}f\wto\mathcal N(0,\sigma_f^2)$. Bounded in probability, nothing to do.

Two functions

$\max(|\mathbb G^{(n)}f_1|,|\mathbb G^{(n)}f_2|)$ converges to the max of two (correlated) normals. Still bounded. A maximum of finitely many convergent things converges.

$N$ functions

Still fine for fixed $N$, but the size now depends on $N$: the maximum of $N$ standard normals is roughly $\sqrt{2\log N}$, and this grows without bound as $N$ grows. So a bound that is uniform over a family must contain a price that depends on how many functions there effectively are. §4 derives that price.

Infinitely many functions

Now it can genuinely fail. Take $Z_i$ uniform on $[0,1]$ and $$\mathcal F=\{\mathbf 1_A:A\subset[0,1]\ \text{finite}\}.$$ For each fixed finite $A$ we have $P(A)=0$ and, almost surely, no data point lands in $A$, so $\mathbb P^{(n)}\mathbf 1_A=0$ and $\mathbb G^{(n)}\mathbf 1_A=0$. Every individual function behaves perfectly. But now choose $A$ after seeing the data, namely $A=\{Z_1,\dots,Z_n\}$, which is finite and so belongs to $\mathcal F$. Then $\mathbb P^{(n)}\mathbf 1_A=1$ while $P\mathbf 1_A=0$, so $$\big\|\mathbb G^{(n)}\big\|_{\mathcal F}\ \ge\ \sqrt n\,(1-0)=\sqrt n\ \longrightarrow\ \infty .$$

The lesson to extract

Pointwise information is worth nothing for a supremum, because the supremum is allowed to pick its $f$ after looking at the data. Any argument that is going to work must therefore control all $f$ simultaneously and in advance, using inequalities that hold for every possible dataset. Hold onto that phrase: it is exactly what a bracket delivers and what an “approximation on average” does not.

So the plan must be: reduce the infinite family to a finite one, in advance, with inequalities valid for all data; pay the $\sqrt{\log N}$ price for the finite maximum; and control the error made by the reduction. Three tools, one per §3, §4, §5.

§3Tool 1 — how likely is one average to be far out?

The CLT tells us $\mathbb G^{(n)}f$ is approximately normal, but a limit statement is not enough: to take a maximum over $N$ things we need a bound valid at finite $n$, and we need to know how the bound depends on $f$. That is Bernstein's inequality, the only result on this page quoted without proof.

Bernstein's inequality — quoted (Theorem 1.3 in the notes)

Let $g$ satisfy $|g|\le M$ and $Pg=0$, and write $\sigma^2=\|g\|^2_{L_2(P)}$. Then for every $x>0$, $$\mathbb P\Big(\big|\mathbb G^{(n)}g\big|>x\Big)\;\le\;2\exp\left(-\frac{\tfrac12x^2}{\sigma^2+\tfrac13Mx/\sqrt n}\right).$$

Two readings, and both matter later.

  • For moderate $x$ the second term in the denominator is negligible and the bound is $2e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}$ — exactly a normal tail with standard deviation $\sigma$. So we may think of $\mathbb G^{(n)}g$ as “a normal with standard deviation $\|g\|_{L_2}$”, uniformly in $n$.
  • The parameter controlling the tail is $\|g\|_{L_2(P)}$. Not $\|g\|_{L_1}$, not the sup norm. This is not a stylistic choice: the variance of $\mathbb G^{(n)}g$ is $\operatorname{Var}(g(Z_1))\le\|g\|_{L_2}^2$, computed in §1. If you want to say “this piece of the process is small”, the quantity you must make small is its $L_2$ norm.
Answer to “why is entropy measured in $L_2$?”

Because the fluctuation size of $\mathbb G^{(n)}g$ is $\|g\|_{L_2(P)}$, and that is the only thing the tail bound sees. Later, when we approximate $f$ by a bracket endpoint, the leftover is some $g$, and the leftover's contribution to the process is governed by $\|g\|_{L_2}$. So brackets must be measured in $L_2$ — otherwise we would be controlling a quantity that does not appear in the bound we intend to use.

§4Tool 2 — what a maximum over $N$ costs

Now the derivation of $\sqrt{\log N}$, in full. Let $X_1,\dots,X_N$ be any random variables — not necessarily independent — each satisfying the normal-type tail

$$\mathbb P(|X_j|>x)\le2e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}\qquad\text{for all }x>0 .$$

The event that the maximum exceeds $x$ is the union of the $N$ events that some particular $X_j$ exceeds $x$. The union bound (probability of a union $\le$ sum of probabilities — no independence needed) gives

$$\mathbb P\Big(\max_{j\le N}|X_j|>x\Big)\;\le\;\sum_{j=1}^N\mathbb P(|X_j|>x)\;\le\;2N e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}.$$

Now choose $x$ so that the right-hand side is small. Set $x=\sigma\sqrt{2\log(2N/\alpha)}$; then $2Ne^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}=2Ne^{-\log(2N/\alpha)}=\alpha$. In words:

The maximal inequality, derived

$$\max_{j\le N}|X_j|\;\le\;\sigma\sqrt{2\log(2N/\alpha)}\qquad\text{with probability at least }1-\alpha,$$ and integrating the tail gives the expectation form $\mathbb E\big[\max_{j\le N}|X_j|\big]\le C\sigma\sqrt{\log N}$ for a universal constant $C$.

Look carefully at the shape, because the whole theory is a consequence of it:

  • The cost of a maximum enters through $\sqrt{\log N}$ — you can afford enormous $N$. Going from $10$ functions to $10^{6}$ multiplies the bound by only $\sqrt{6}\approx2.4$.
  • The cost is multiplied by $\sigma$, the common $L_2$ size. So a maximum over many small pieces is small: $N$ pieces each of $L_2$ size $\varepsilon$ contribute about $\varepsilon\sqrt{\log N}$. Keep this product in view — it is the integrand of the entropy integral, and it is the only thing the integral is made of.

§5Tool 3 — turning infinitely many functions into $N$ of them

We need to replace $\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}$ by $\max_{j\le N}$. The obvious idea is to pick representatives and hope the rest are close. Let us try it honestly and see it fail; the failure tells us what to do instead.

5.1  The naive attempt, and why it does not close

Pick $f_1,\dots,f_N$ such that every $f\in\mathcal F$ has some $f_j$ with $\|f-f_j\|_{L_2}\le\varepsilon$. Then for that $f$, $$\big|\mathbb G^{(n)}f\big|\;\le\;\big|\mathbb G^{(n)}f_j\big|+\big|\mathbb G^{(n)}(f-f_j)\big| .$$ The first term is fine: a maximum over $N$ things, §4. The second term has small $L_2$ norm, so by §3 each single instance of it is small. But we need it small for every $f$ at once, and the collection $\{f-f_{j(f)}:f\in\mathcal F\}$ is still an infinite family. We have not reduced anything: we are trying to bound a supremum over an infinite family using a bound for one member at a time, which §2 showed is exactly the thing that does not work.

The precise defect

“$\|f-f_j\|_{L_2}$ is small” is a statement about an average over $z$. The supremum is allowed to choose $f$ after seeing the data, so it will choose the $f$ whose deviation happens to sit exactly on the observed points. An averaged guarantee cannot rule that out. We need a guarantee that holds at every $z$ separately, so that it survives being evaluated at whatever the data turn out to be.

5.2  What the defect forces: a pointwise two-sided cage

So demand approximation from below and above, valid at every point: $$l_j(z)\;\le\;f(z)\;\le\;u_j(z)\qquad\text{for every }z\text{ and every }f\in\mathcal F_j .$$ A pair $[l_j,u_j]$ is called a bracket, and $\mathcal F_j$ is the set of functions caught inside it. That is all the word means: a floor and a ceiling that no member of the group is allowed to cross, anywhere.

Why this is the right object becomes visible the moment you use it. The point is that inequalities between functions survive integration against any measure — including the random empirical one.

The key derivation. Everything after this is bookkeeping.

Let $l\le f\le u$ pointwise. Then $f-l\ge0$ and $f-l\le u-l$ pointwise, so applying both $\mathbb P^{(n)}$ and $P$ — each of which is a positive linear operator, i.e. preserves “$\ge0$” — gives four inequalities: $$0\le\mathbb P^{(n)}(f-l)\le\mathbb P^{(n)}(u-l),\qquad 0\le P(f-l)\le P(u-l).$$ Now write the difference of processes and substitute: $$\mathbb G^{(n)}f-\mathbb G^{(n)}l=\sqrt n\Big(\mathbb P^{(n)}(f-l)-P(f-l)\Big).$$ Upper bound: keep the largest possible first term and the smallest possible second term, $$\mathbb G^{(n)}f-\mathbb G^{(n)}l\;\le\;\sqrt n\,\mathbb P^{(n)}(u-l)\;=\;\underbrace{\mathbb G^{(n)}(u-l)}_{\text{random, mean }0}+\underbrace{\sqrt n\,P(u-l)}_{\text{deterministic}} .$$ Lower bound: smallest first term, largest second term, $$\mathbb G^{(n)}f-\mathbb G^{(n)}l\;\ge\;-\sqrt n\,P(u-l).$$ Combining, $$\boxed{\ \sup_{f\in[l,u]}\big|\mathbb G^{(n)}f-\mathbb G^{(n)}l\big|\;\le\;\big|\mathbb G^{(n)}(u-l)\big|\;+\;\sqrt n\,P(u-l).\ }$$

Read the box slowly, because it is the entire mechanism of the subject.

  • The supremum on the left is over infinitely many $f$; the right-hand side does not mention $f$ at all. It involves only the two fixed functions $l$ and $u$. That is the reduction we needed, and it works because the cage is pointwise.
  • Two costs appear, and they pull in the same direction. A random cost $|\mathbb G^{(n)}(u-l)|$, whose size is $\|u-l\|_{L_2}$ by §3, and a deterministic cost $\sqrt n\,P(u-l)$, i.e. $\sqrt n$ times the $L_1$ size. Both shrink when the bracket is tighter.
  • The two costs are measured in different norms. $L_2$ for the random one, $L_1$ for the deterministic one. Keep both in view; §7 is entirely about the fact that the second one carries a factor $\sqrt n$.
Definition, now unavoidable rather than arbitrary

A bracket $[l,u]$ is an $\varepsilon$-bracket in $L_r(P)$ if $\|u-l\|_{L_r}\le\varepsilon$. The bracketing number $N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_r(P))$ is the smallest number of $\varepsilon$-brackets needed to cover $\mathcal F$. That is the $N$ that will go into the maximal inequality of §4.

§6A complete proof, with no gaps: the uniform law of large numbers

Before touching $\sqrt n$, let us assemble the three tools into a full theorem at the easier scale. Everything below is derived; nothing is quoted except the ordinary strong law.

Theorem (Glivenko–Cantelli, bracketing form — Theorem 1.18 in the notes)

If $N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_1(P))<\infty$ for every $\varepsilon>0$, then $$\big\|\mathbb P^{(n)}-P\big\|_{\mathcal F}=\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\big|\mathbb P^{(n)}f-Pf\big|\;\longrightarrow\;0\quad\text{almost surely.}$$

Proof. Fix $\varepsilon>0$. By assumption there are finitely many brackets $[l_1,u_1],\dots,[l_N,u_N]$ with $P(u_j-l_j)\le\varepsilon$ whose union covers $\mathcal F$. Take any $f\in\mathcal F$ and let $j$ be an index with $l_j\le f\le u_j$.

Upper bound. Since $f\le u_j$ pointwise, $\mathbb P^{(n)}f\le\mathbb P^{(n)}u_j$. Add and subtract $Pu_j$: $$\mathbb P^{(n)}f-Pf\;\le\;\mathbb P^{(n)}u_j-Pf\;=\;\big(\mathbb P^{(n)}u_j-Pu_j\big)+\big(Pu_j-Pf\big).$$ For the last bracket use $f\ge l_j$ pointwise, so $Pu_j-Pf\le Pu_j-Pl_j=P(u_j-l_j)\le\varepsilon$. Hence $$\mathbb P^{(n)}f-Pf\;\le\;\big(\mathbb P^{(n)}-P\big)u_j+\varepsilon .$$

Lower bound. Symmetrically, $\mathbb P^{(n)}f\ge\mathbb P^{(n)}l_j$ and $Pf\le Pu_j$, so $$\mathbb P^{(n)}f-Pf\;\ge\;\big(\mathbb P^{(n)}-P\big)l_j-\varepsilon .$$

Combine. Both bounds are indexed only by $j$, so taking the supremum over $f$ costs nothing: $$\big\|\mathbb P^{(n)}-P\big\|_{\mathcal F}\;\le\;\max_{j\le N}\Big\{\big|\big(\mathbb P^{(n)}-P\big)u_j\big|,\ \big|\big(\mathbb P^{(n)}-P\big)l_j\big|\Big\}+\varepsilon .$$ The maximum is over $2N$ fixed functions, and for each one the strong law gives $(\mathbb P^{(n)}-P)u_j\to0$ almost surely. A maximum of finitely many sequences each tending to $0$ tends to $0$. Therefore $$\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big\|\mathbb P^{(n)}-P\big\|_{\mathcal F}\le\varepsilon\quad\text{a.s.}$$ Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, intersecting countably many almost-sure events over $\varepsilon=1/k$ finishes the proof. $\square$

What this proof teaches, and what it hides

It contains the full mechanism — cage, cover, finite maximum, residual $\varepsilon$ — and it is completely rigorous. Note where each tool went: §5's pointwise cage let the supremum be dropped, and the finite maximum was free because at this scale each term goes to $0$ on its own. The maximal inequality of §4 was never needed: a maximum of $N$ null sequences is null no matter how large $N$ is. That is the luxury we are about to lose.

§7Now multiply by $\sqrt n$, and watch exactly what breaks

Repeat §6 verbatim at the CLT scale. The box from §5.2 already did the algebra: for $f$ in bracket $j$, $$\big|\mathbb G^{(n)}f-\mathbb G^{(n)}l_j\big|\;\le\;\underbrace{\big|\mathbb G^{(n)}(u_j-l_j)\big|}_{\text{(A) fluctuation}}\;+\;\underbrace{\sqrt n\,P(u_j-l_j)}_{\text{(B) bias}} .$$ So the whole process is within (A)+(B) of a process indexed by only $N$ fixed functions. Now size the two terms for the model case: the half-line class, with brackets built from cells of probability mass $m$. Then $u_j-l_j$ is the indicator of a cell, so by §1

$$\|u_j-l_j\|_{L_2}=\sqrt m,\qquad P(u_j-l_j)=m .$$

Using §4 with $\sigma=\sqrt m$ and §3's normal-type tail, and $N\approx1/m$ brackets:

$$\text{(A)}\ \approx\ \sqrt m\,\sqrt{2\log N}=\sqrt m\,\sqrt{2\log(1/m)},\qquad\qquad \text{(B)}\ =\ \sqrt n\,m .$$

7.1  The conflict, in numbers

Here is why that is fatal, stated precisely. What we must prove is asymptotic equicontinuity: for every $\eta>0$, $$\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}\ \limsup_{n\to\infty}\ \mathbb P\Big(\sup_{\|f-g\|_{L_2}<\delta}\big|\mathbb G^{(n)}f-\mathbb G^{(n)}g\big|>\eta\Big)=0 .$$ Read the order of the limits: $\delta$ is fixed first, then $n\to\infty$, and only afterwards does $\delta$ shrink. That order is not negotiable — it is what “the process does not develop fine-scale wiggles, uniformly in $n$” means. And the single-level bound contains the term $\sqrt n\,P(u-l)$, in which $P(u-l)$ depends on $\delta$ but not on $n$. So for every fixed $\delta$, the bound tends to $\infty$ as $n\to\infty$. The bound is useless, no matter how the constants are tuned.

And you cannot escape by letting the brackets shrink with $n$

Choosing $m=m_n\to0$ fast enough kills (B), but then the finite family you compared against is $N_n\to\infty$ functions that change with $n$ — no longer a fixed finite-dimensional object, so you can no longer conclude anything from convergence of finite-dimensional distributions. You have moved the problem, not solved it.

7.2  Where the crudeness is

Look back at the derivation in §5.2 and find the wasteful step. It is $$\mathbb P^{(n)}(f-l)\;\le\;\mathbb P^{(n)}(u-l).$$ We replaced the actual leftover $f-l$ by the worst possible leftover $u-l$, at every data point simultaneously. For a typical $f$ sitting in the middle of the bracket, that is a gross overestimate — and we then paid $\sqrt n$ times it. The repair is not to be cleverer about one bracket, but to never take a large step at all.

§8The repair: many resolutions, and where the integral comes from

The idea is called chaining. Instead of approximating $f$ once by a coarse endpoint and paying for the whole leftover, approximate it repeatedly, at finer and finer resolutions, and pay only for the small corrections between consecutive levels.

8.1  The telescope

Fix the dyadic scales $\varepsilon_m=2^{-m}$ for $m=0,1,2,\dots$ At each level, cover $\mathcal F$ by $N_m:=N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon_m,\mathcal F,L_2(P))$ brackets, and let $\pi_mf$ denote the endpoint of the level-$m$ bracket containing $f$. Then for any $M$, $$\mathbb G^{(n)}f-\mathbb G^{(n)}\pi_0f\;=\;\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Big(\mathbb G^{(n)}\pi_mf-\mathbb G^{(n)}\pi_{m-1}f\Big)\;+\;\Big(\mathbb G^{(n)}f-\mathbb G^{(n)}\pi_Mf\Big).$$ This is an identity — everything cancels. Its value is that each increment $\pi_mf-\pi_{m-1}f$ is small: both endpoints approximate the same $f$ at neighbouring resolutions, so their $L_2$ distance is at most $\varepsilon_{m-1}+\varepsilon_m\le3\varepsilon_m$. And there are at most $N_mN_{m-1}\le N_m^2$ distinct such increments, because each is determined by which bracket $f$ falls into at levels $m$ and $m-1$.

Apply §4 to level $m$: a maximum over $N_m^2$ terms of common $L_2$ size $3\varepsilon_m$ costs

$$\mathbb E\Big[\max_{\text{level-}m\text{ increments}}\big|\mathbb G^{(n)}(\cdot)\big|\Big]\;\lesssim\;\varepsilon_m\sqrt{\log N_m^2}\;\asymp\;\varepsilon_m\sqrt{\log N_m}.$$

Summing over levels, the total cost of the entire chain is

$$\sum_{m\ge1}\varepsilon_m\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon_m)}\;=\;\sum_{m\ge1}2^{-m}\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(2^{-m})} .$$

8.2  That sum is the integral

Now the punchline, and it is an elementary comparison. $N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)$ is non-increasing in $\varepsilon$, so for $\varepsilon\in[2^{-m-1},2^{-m}]$ we have $\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)}\ge\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(2^{-m})}$. That interval has length $2^{-m-1}$, so $$\int_{2^{-m-1}}^{2^{-m}}\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)}\,d\varepsilon\;\ge\;2^{-m-1}\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(2^{-m})}\;=\;\tfrac12\,\varepsilon_m\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon_m)} .$$ Summing over $m\ge1$, the intervals are disjoint and fill $(0,\tfrac12]$, so $$\sum_{m\ge1}\varepsilon_m\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon_m)}\;\le\;2\int_0^{1/2}\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)}\,d\varepsilon\;\le\;2\int_0^{1}\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)}\,d\varepsilon .$$

This is the answer to “where does the entropy integral come from?”

It is the total bill for the chain: the sum over resolutions of (size of the correction at that resolution) × (price of a maximum at that resolution), and the dyadic sum is within a factor $2$ of $$\int_0^1\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2(P))}\;d\varepsilon .$$ Finite integral means the chain converges: the fine corrections are summable, so the process is uniformly close to a finite-dimensional one, uniformly in $n$. That is asymptotic equicontinuity, and with convergence of the finite-dimensional distributions it is weak convergence.

8.3  What is still being hidden, stated plainly

The bias term of §7 has not vanished by magic; it has been made affordable. At each level the argument does not use the crude step “replace the leftover by the whole bracket width”. Instead the increment is split into a part bounded by a threshold — where Bernstein's inequality is in its sub-Gaussian regime and produces the clean $\varepsilon_m\sqrt{\log N_m}$ above — and a rare part where the function is large, which is handled by a moment bound and contributes at higher order. The thresholds are chosen level by level, roughly as $\sqrt n\,\varepsilon_m/\sqrt{\log N_m}$, which is exactly the point at which the second term in Bernstein's denominator stops mattering. Carrying this bookkeeping through is the content of the proof of Theorem 1.19; it is in van der Vaart, Theorem 19.5, and it is not examinable.

What is examinable, and what you should now feel is inevitable rather than arbitrary, is the statement:

Theorem 1.19 — the Bracketing CLT

If $$\int_0^1\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}\big(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2(P)\big)}\;d\varepsilon\;<\;\infty ,$$ then $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Donsker: $\mathbb G^{(n)}=\sqrt n(\mathbb P^{(n)}-P)$ converges weakly in $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$ to a tight Gaussian process $\mathbb G$ with $\mathbb E[\mathbb G f]=0$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbb Gf,\mathbb Gg)=Pfg-Pf\,Pg$.

How forgiving the condition is — and why you will never lose a mark here

Suppose $N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)\le C\varepsilon^{-K}$ for some constants. Then $\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)\le\log C+K\log(1/\varepsilon)$ and $$\int_0^1\sqrt{\log C+K\log(1/\varepsilon)}\;d\varepsilon<\infty ,$$ because $\log(1/\varepsilon)$ is integrable on $(0,1]$ — indeed $\int_0^1\log(1/\varepsilon)d\varepsilon=1$ — and the square root only makes it smaller near $0$ where the integrand exceeds $1$. So: any polynomial bracketing number, of any degree, passes. In practice the last step of every proof is this one sentence, and it is identical every time.

§9What you actually have to do

Everything above says the theory has exactly one input: a bound on $N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2(P))$. So an application has exactly five steps, and only steps 2 and 3 involve any thought.

  1. Name the family and its parameter. $\mathcal F=\{f_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}$. Say what $\Theta$ is and its dimension $d$; expect $N\approx(\text{something})^d$.
  2. Build the cage on a cell, and check it is a cage. Choose cells in $\Theta$ of an unnamed fineness, and write $l_j,u_j$ with a one-line reason why every $f_\theta$ with $\theta$ in the cell satisfies $l_j\le f_\theta\le u_j$ pointwise. Two mechanisms exist, and they exhaust the course:
    ORDER — if $\theta\mapsto f_\theta(z)$ is monotone for each fixed $z$, take $l_j,u_j$ to be the family evaluated at the two ends of the cell. The cage is then exact, by monotonicity.
    SIZE — if instead $|f_\theta-f_{\theta'}|\le\|\theta-\theta'\|\,m(z)$ for an envelope $m$ with $\|m\|_{L_2}<\infty$, take $l_j=f_{\theta_j}-\delta m$ and $u_j=f_{\theta_j}+\delta m$ for a cell of diameter $\delta$ around $\theta_j$.
  3. Compute the gap and its $L_2$ norm as a formula in the fineness. Write $u_j-l_j$ explicitly, then $\|u_j-l_j\|_{L_2}$. If the gap is an indicator, §1 says this is $\sqrt{\text{mass}}$; otherwise compute directly.
  4. Solve $\|u_j-l_j\|_{L_2}\le\varepsilon$ for the fineness, and count. This is where $\varepsilon^2$ appears, or fails to — it is an algebraic consequence of step 3, never a choice. Then $N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)=$ (size of $\Theta$) / (fineness), to the power $d$.
  5. Say the invariant sentence. “$N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)$ is polynomial in $1/\varepsilon$, so the entropy integral converges and Theorem 1.19 applies.”
The one refinement that step 3 sometimes needs

If the gap is an indicator of a set built from two constraints — a union of two strips, as happens whenever $\Theta$ is two-dimensional — then bound its mass by a union bound and give each constraint half the budget: mass $\le\varepsilon^2/2+\varepsilon^2/2=\varepsilon^2$. Nothing else changes, and the count gets squared. Examples 3, 4 and 5 below all do exactly this.

§10Twelve examples, with every line of algebra

Same five steps every time. Read two or three carefully and the pattern will start doing the work for you; the point of having twelve is that you can check the pattern rather than take it on trust.

01

Half-lines: $\mathcal F=\{\mathbf 1_{[0,t]}:t\ge0\}$

Survival indicators: $\mathcal F=\{\mathbf 1_{(t,\infty)}:t\ge0\}$

Mechanism. $\mathbf 1_{(t,\infty)}=1-\mathbf 1_{[0,t]}$: monotone decreasing in $t$. ORDER, with the roles of the two ends swapped.

05

Rectangles in the plane: $\mathcal F=\{\mathbf 1_{(a,b]}(x)\mathbf 1_{(c,d]}(y)\}$

Parameter. Four numbers $(a,b,c,d)$. Expect a fourth power.

Cage. Largest rectangle in the cell against smallest, exactly as in example 3 done twice: shrink $a,c$ to their left cell-ends and grow $b,d$ to their right cell-ends for $u$; the reverse for $l$.

Gap. An indicator of a region contained in the union of four strips — one per parameter.

Solve. Union bound over four terms, so each strip gets budget $\varepsilon^2/4$: $$\|u-l\|_{L_2}\le\big(4\cdot\varepsilon^2/4\big)^{1/2}=\varepsilon .$$ Each margin needs $\le\lceil8/\varepsilon^2\rceil$ grid points at that budget.

Count. Two cell-choices per margin, so $N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)\le\lceil8/\varepsilon^2\rceil^4=O(\varepsilon^{-8})$. Polynomial. Donsker. $\square$

The general rule this example is here to display: $k$ monotone constraints ⇒ split the budget $k$ ways ⇒ the count is raised to the power $k$ ⇒ $\log N_{[\,]}$ picks up a factor $k$ ⇒ nothing whatsoever changes about convergence. This is why dimension is cheap for rectangle-type classes and expensive for, say, convex sets, where the number of constraints is not bounded.

Mechanism. Still monotone in $t$ because $w\ge0$ (this is where non-negativity is used — with a sign-changing $w$ you would take pointwise min and max instead, as in example 10). ORDER.

Cage. $l_j=w\cdot\mathbf 1_{[0,t_{j-1}]}$, $u_j=w\cdot\mathbf 1_{[0,t_j)}$.

Gap. $u_j-l_j=w\cdot\mathbf 1_{(t_{j-1},t_j)}$ — an indicator weighted by $w$, so no longer an indicator.

So — following the rule — cut the axis of the measure that appears: build the grid so that $\mu(\text{cell})\le\varepsilon^2$, which is possible because $\mu$ is a finite measure with total mass $\mu(\mathbb R)=\|w\|_{L_2}^2<\infty$.

07

Excess functions: $\mathcal F=\{z\mapsto(z-u)^+:u\in[0,U]\}$, assuming $\mathbb E[Z_1^2]<\infty$

Mechanism. $u\mapsto(z-u)^+$ is decreasing in $u$ for every $z$. ORDER.

Cage. For $u\in[u_{j-1},u_j)$ with $u_j-u_{j-1}=\delta$: $l_j(z)=(z-u_j)^+$, $u_j^{\rm br}(z)=(z-u_{j-1})^+$.

Gap. $u_j^{\rm br}-l_j=(z-u_{j-1})^+-(z-u_j)^+$. The function $x\mapsto(z-x)^+$ is $1$-Lipschitz, so this is between $0$ and $u_j-u_{j-1}=\delta$, pointwise.

Its $L_2$ norm. A function bounded by the constant $\delta$ has $L_2$ norm at most $\delta$: $$\|u_j^{\rm br}-l_j\|_{L_2}=\big(\mathbb E[(\cdots)^2]\big)^{1/2}\le\big(\mathbb E[\delta^2]\big)^{1/2}=\delta .$$

Solve. $\delta\le\varepsilon$, so take $\delta=\varepsilon$. No square appears, because the gap was not an indicator — there was no $\mathbf 1^2=\mathbf 1$ collapse.

Count. $N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)\le\lceil U/\varepsilon\rceil$. Polynomial. Donsker. $\square$

The second moment is needed not for the brackets but so that the class has a square-integrable envelope, i.e. so that the limiting Gaussian process has finite variances at all. Bracketing controls complexity; integrability is a separate hypothesis.

08

Truncations: $\mathcal F=\{z\mapsto\min(z,c):c\in[0,C]\}$

Mechanism. Increasing in $c$. ORDER.

Cage. $l_j(z)=\min(z,c_{j-1})$, $u_j(z)=\min(z,c_j)$, on cells of length $\delta$.

Gap. $\min(z,c_j)-\min(z,c_{j-1})\in[0,\delta]$ pointwise, since $c\mapsto\min(z,c)$ is $1$-Lipschitz.

$L_2$ norm, solve, count. $\le\delta$; set $\delta=\varepsilon$; $N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)\le\lceil C/\varepsilon\rceil$. Donsker, with no moment assumption at all — the whole class is bounded by $C$. $\square$

09

Exponential weights: $\mathcal F=\{z\mapsto e^{-\lambda z}:\lambda\in[0,1]\}$ on $z\ge0$, assuming $\mathbb E[Z_1^2]<\infty$

Mechanism. Decreasing in $\lambda$ for $z\ge0$. ORDER — use it even though a Lipschitz bound also exists, because ORDER is exact and gives a smaller count.

Cage. $l_j(z)=e^{-\lambda_jz}$, $u_j(z)=e^{-\lambda_{j-1}z}$ on cells of length $\delta$.

Gap. Factorise: $$e^{-\lambda_{j-1}z}-e^{-\lambda_jz}=e^{-\lambda_{j-1}z}\big(1-e^{-(\lambda_j-\lambda_{j-1})z}\big)\le1\cdot\big(1-e^{-\delta z}\big)\le\delta z,$$ using $e^{-\lambda_{j-1}z}\le1$ and $1-e^{-x}\le x$.

Its $L_2$ norm. $\|u_j-l_j\|_{L_2}\le\delta\big(\mathbb E[Z_1^2]\big)^{1/2}=\delta\|Z_1\|_{L_2}$.

Solve. $\delta\|Z_1\|_{L_2}\le\varepsilon\iff\delta=\varepsilon/\|Z_1\|_{L_2}$.

Count. $N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)\le\lceil\|Z_1\|_{L_2}/\varepsilon\rceil$. Polynomial. Donsker. $\square$

10

A one-parameter linear class: $\mathcal F=\{z\mapsto\theta z:\theta\in[-1,1]\}$, $\mathbb E[Z_1^2]<\infty$

Mechanism. For fixed $z$, $\theta\mapsto\theta z$ is monotone — but increasing when $z>0$ and decreasing when $z<0$. So ORDER still applies, provided you build the cage from whichever endpoint is smaller at that $z$: $$l_j(z)=\min\big(\theta_{j-1}z,\;\theta_jz\big),\qquad u_j(z)=\max\big(\theta_{j-1}z,\;\theta_jz\big).$$ These are legitimate functions of $z$ and the cage holds pointwise because a linear function of $\theta$ on an interval attains its extremes at the ends. ✓

Gap. $u_j-l_j=|\theta_j-\theta_{j-1}|\,|z|=\delta|z|$.

Its $L_2$ norm. $\delta\,\|Z_1\|_{L_2}$.

Solve. $\delta=\varepsilon/\|Z_1\|_{L_2}$.

Count. The parameter interval has length $2$, so $N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)\le\lceil2\|Z_1\|_{L_2}/\varepsilon\rceil$. Donsker. $\square$

This is the moment where “ORDER” and “SIZE” meet: taking min and max over the corners of the parameter cell is the general recipe, and for a Lipschitz family it reproduces the padded brackets $f_{\theta_j}\pm\delta m$ up to a constant.

11

An affine class: $\mathcal F=\{z\mapsto a+bz:(a,b)\in[-1,1]^2\}$

Parameter. $d=2$. Expect $\varepsilon^{-2}$.

Cage. On a rectangle cell $[a_{j-1},a_j]\times[b_{k-1},b_k]$ with side lengths $\Delta_a,\Delta_b$, take the pointwise min and max over the four corners. Since $a+bz$ is affine in $(a,b)$, the extremes over the rectangle are attained at corners, so the cage is exact.

Gap. Bound it by moving one parameter at a time: $$u-l\;\le\;\underbrace{\Delta_a}_{\text{from }a}+\underbrace{\Delta_b|z|}_{\text{from }b}.$$

Its $L_2$ norm. By the triangle inequality in $L_2$, $\|u-l\|_{L_2}\le\Delta_a+\Delta_b\|Z_1\|_{L_2}$.

Solve. Split the budget: $\Delta_a=\varepsilon/2$ and $\Delta_b=\varepsilon/(2\|Z_1\|_{L_2})$ gives $\|u-l\|_{L_2}\le\varepsilon$. Same budget-splitting move as examples 3–5, now for two different units rather than two strips.

Count. $\lceil2/\Delta_a\rceil\cdot\lceil2/\Delta_b\rceil\le C/\varepsilon^2$. Polynomial. Donsker. $\square$

12

An oscillating class: $\mathcal F=\{z\mapsto\sin(\theta z):\theta\in[0,T]\}$, $\mathbb E[Z_1^2]<\infty$

Mechanism. Now genuinely not monotone in $\theta$: as $\theta$ grows, $\sin(\theta z)$ oscillates. ORDER is unavailable, so use SIZE.

Lipschitz bound. By the mean value theorem applied to $\theta\mapsto\sin(\theta z)$, whose derivative is $z\cos(\theta z)$ and is bounded by $|z|$: $$|\sin(\theta_1z)-\sin(\theta_2z)|\le|\theta_1-\theta_2|\,|z| ,$$ so the envelope is $m(z)=|z|$ with $\|m\|_{L_2}=\|Z_1\|_{L_2}<\infty$. ✓

Cage. On a cell of diameter $\delta$ with centre $\theta_j$: $l_j=\sin(\theta_jz)-\delta|z|$ and $u_j=\sin(\theta_jz)+\delta|z|$. The cage holds pointwise by the displayed Lipschitz bound.

Gap. $u_j-l_j=2\delta|z|$.

$L_2$ norm, solve, count. $2\delta\|Z_1\|_{L_2}\le\varepsilon$ gives $\delta=\varepsilon/(2\|Z_1\|_{L_2})$, so $N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)\le\lceil2T\|Z_1\|_{L_2}/\varepsilon\rceil$. Polynomial. Donsker. $\square$

The same computation with $e^{i\theta z}$ in place of $\sin(\theta z)$, using $|e^{i\theta_1z}-e^{i\theta_2z}|\le|\theta_1-\theta_2||z|$, gives the empirical characteristic function on $[-T,T]$.

§11Drills

Work on paper first: name the parameter, choose the mechanism, write the cage and justify it pointwise, compute the gap's $L_2$ norm as a formula in the fineness, solve, count.

Drill 1.  $\mathcal F=\{\mathbf 1_{[0,t]}\}$ when $Z_1$ is discrete, say uniform on $\{1,2,3\}$. Does anything break?

No. Take $\varepsilon=0.4$, so $\varepsilon^2=0.16$, and build the grid by $t_j=\inf\{t:F(t)\ge0.16j\}$. Since $F$ jumps by $1/3$ at each of $1,2,3$, the grid points come out as $t_1=t_2=1$, $t_3=t_4=2$, and so on: consecutive grid points coincide. The cells between coinciding points are empty and carry mass $0\le\varepsilon^2$; the cells that straddle an atom have mass $F(t_j-)-F(t_{j-1})=0$ as well, because the atom sits at the grid point and the open cell excludes it. Every cell satisfies the requirement, the count is still at most $\lceil2/\varepsilon^2\rceil$, and the proof runs unchanged. This is exactly what the left limit $F(t_j-)$ in example 1 is for.

Drill 2.  $\mathcal F=\{z\mapsto|z-\theta|:\theta\in[0,1]\}$.

Not monotone in $\theta$ (it decreases then increases), so SIZE. The reverse triangle inequality gives $\big||z-\theta_1|-|z-\theta_2|\big|\le|\theta_1-\theta_2|$, so the envelope is the constant $m\equiv1$ and $\|m\|_{L_2}=1$. Polynomial, Donsker, and no moment assumption is needed for the brackets — though $\mathbb E[Z_1^2]<\infty$ is needed for the class to have square-integrable envelope.

Drill 4.  Why is the class of all indicators of finite subsets of $[0,1]$ not covered by any of this?

Try to build a cage. Any bracket containing $\mathbf 1_A$ for every finite $A$ must have $u\ge\mathbf 1_A$ for all of them, hence $u\equiv1$ on $[0,1]$, while $l\le\mathbf 1_\emptyset=0$ gives $l\le0$. So the only bracket covering more than a negligible part of the class has $\|u-l\|_{L_2}=1$, and for $\varepsilon<1$ you would need one bracket per function — infinitely many. Thus $N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)=\infty$ and the theorem simply does not apply, in agreement with §2 where the supremum was computed to be $\sqrt n$.

§12The whole page in one box

What was proved, in order
  1. $\mathbb G^{(n)}f$ is a normalised sum; its fluctuation size is $\|f\|_{L_2(P)}$ (§1, §3).
  2. A maximum over $N$ such things costs a factor $\sqrt{\log N}$, by the union bound (§4).
  3. A supremum over infinitely many cannot be handled by averaged approximation, because the supremum sees the data (§2, §5.1). A pointwise cage can, because inequalities survive integration against the empirical measure (§5.2).
  4. Caging plus a finite maximum proves the uniform law of large numbers outright (§6).
  5. At the $\sqrt n$ scale the same argument leaves a term $\sqrt n\,P(u-l)$, which for fixed bracket size diverges. One resolution is not enough (§7).
  6. Approximating across all resolutions instead, the cost is $\sum_m\varepsilon_m\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon_m)}$, which is within a factor $2$ of $\int_0^1\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon)}\,d\varepsilon$ (§8).
  7. Hence the only thing an application must supply is a bound on $N_{[\,]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2(P))$, and any polynomial bound suffices (§8, §9).
  8. Supplying it is five mechanical steps, with two mechanisms and one algebraic move — solve for the fineness (§9, §10).

Numbering of results follows the Week 1 chapter. The single quoted ingredient is Bernstein's inequality; the single unproved assembly is the truncation-and-chaining bookkeeping inside Theorem 1.19, which is where the $\sqrt n\,P(u-l)$ term of §7 is finally disposed of, and which is not examinable.
